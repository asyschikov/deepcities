In Plan Zuid and Plan West the Amsterdam School means four-storey brick walls. North of the IJ and in the Watergraafsmeer it looks completely different: two-storey cottages under big tiled roofs, little squares, timber cladding, even thatch. These are the *tuindorpen* (garden villages) that the city built for itself in the late 1910s and 1920s.

## The garden-city idea

The English reformer Ebenezer Howard published *To-morrow: a Peaceful Path to Real Reform* in 1898, reissued in 1902 as *Garden Cities of To-morrow*. He wanted new towns that combined the advantages of town and country: low density, green belts and communal ownership, each town largely self-sufficient. In the Netherlands the idea was mostly realised on a smaller scale, as garden villages. English Wikipedia names Tuindorp Oostzaan and Tuindorp Watergraafsmeer as examples.

In Amsterdam the champion was Arie Keppler, director of the Municipal Housing Department (*Gemeentelijke Woningdienst*) from 1915 to 1937. He preferred low-rise family houses to four-storey blocks, inspired by Howard. Low-rise was the rule in the Vogelbuurt in Noord, built up from 1910, and in the emergency villages Vogeldorp and Disteldorp of 1918. Eigen Haard's [blocks by Jan Gratama on the Kievitstraat](place:Gratama's blocks, Kievitstraat and Spechtstraat) (completed 1920) show the type: two storeys under deep mansard roofs.

## Bathhouses first

The new cottages usually had no bathroom, so the city added public bathhouses. For Disteldorp, B.T. Boeyinga designed the tiny [Badhuis Kromme Distelstraat](place:) (1918–1919), with eight showers. Because the area still bordered farmland, he gave it the pyramid roof of a North Holland farmhouse. In Tuindorp Oostzaan, Public Works added the [Badhuis Meteorenweg](place:) (1927), with separate wings for children and for men and women. South of the IJ, the round [Badhuis Boerhaaveplein (Badhuistheater)](place:) (1921) served the new association blocks of Oost.

## Tuindorp Oostzaan: a village for the shipyards

After the First World War the shipyards on the north bank of the IJ grew fast, and in 1919 the Nederlandsche Scheepsbouw Maatschappij moved there too. Housing the workers close to the yards saved building an expensive link across the IJ. From 1919 the Woningdienst built Tuindorp Oostzaan to a plan by B.T. Boeyinga and J.H. Mulder. The state paid for it under the Emergency Housing Act of 1917. The first homes stood on concrete slabs with timber frames and were meant to last no more than 35 years. By 1924 the part north of the Meteorenweg was finished, with 1,324 homes.

Bricks and bricklayers were scarce, so the Woningdienst also experimented. Around the Castorplein, Mulder built [rows of concrete test houses](place:Concrete test houses, Castorplein) using the English Winget block system, and the fire-engine shed on the Kometensingel was cast in concrete as well.

What made it a village rather than an estate was its own set of amenities. Along the Kometensingel you can still see the [thatched public reading room, doctor's house, teachers' houses, fire shed and a nursery school for 240 children](place:Tuindorp Oostzaan civic buildings), and Boeyinga's Mercuriusplein of shops built around a little court. Keppler deliberately left out churches and pubs. Clubs had nowhere to meet until 1932, when the [Zonnehuis](place:) opened as a community hall for a village of about 7,000 people. A preserved 1922 museum house at Meteorenweg 174 shows how residents lived.

## Tuindorp Nieuwendam

Further east, the city built Tuindorp Nieuwendam in 1924–1934, again to an urban plan by Boeyinga and again inspired by Howard. It is often counted among the most beautiful garden villages in the country. Houses have two storeys at most and now came with a bathroom. At its heart lies the octagonal [Purmerplein](place:Purmerplein, Tuindorp Nieuwendam), with shops under continuous awnings. Gate buildings on timber columns lead into short streets of J.H. Mulder's [keukenwoningen](place:Keukenwoningen, Tuindorp Nieuwendam) ("kitchen houses"), so called because their kitchens face the street. The [school on the Wognumerplantsoen](place:Christian school, Wognumerplantsoen) (1925) shows the cubic side of the style. On the Wingerdweg, Hildo Krop's fairy-tale figures decorate the [Wingerdweg schools](place:) (1926).

## Betondorp

In the Watergraafsmeer the shortage of bricks led to the boldest experiment. Between 1923 and 1925 the city built Tuindorp Watergraafsmeer, about 900 homes, as a trial of affordable building in concrete. Ten construction systems were chosen from some fifty entries, and the area soon became known as Betondorp, "Concrete Village". It was the second large Dutch housing project in concrete, after Kossel I in Rotterdam. Jan Gratama and Gerrit Versteeg laid it out along garden-city lines (Gratama had already built the [Kraaipanbuurt (Gratama & Versteeg)](place:) nearby with Versteeg). The streets carry farming names and meet at a central green, the Brink. Not everything worked: some walls let in water, and damp appeared soon after the houses were finished.

Dick Greiner designed the [Brink (Betondorp)](place:) as a single ensemble (1924–1928). It holds shops with flats above, a public reading room and library with "LEESZAAL" in Amsterdam School letters on its door, and a community hall. Greiner is often filed under the Amsterdam School, but Dutch Wikipedia argues that in hindsight he had more in common with the Nieuwe Bouwen, Dutch modernism. The Brink shows both: sculpted towers and lettering on plain, flat-roofed grey blocks.

The writer Gerard Reve grew up here, on the Ploegstraat, from 1924 to 1938. His novel *De Avonden* (*The Evenings*, 1947) turned it into the "Cementwijk". Johan Cruyff was born and raised in Betondorp, across from the Ajax ground. The village was restored in 1987–1989 and became a protected townscape on 20 November 2022.
