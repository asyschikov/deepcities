Most of the Amsterdam School buildings on this map are not villas or offices. They are rented flats for working families, built between about 1915 and 1930. Socialists of the time called the best of them *arbeiderspaleizen*, "workers' palaces". This essay explains how a national law, a crowd of housing associations and a socialist city council paid for that brick, and who actually designed what.

## The Housing Act of 1901

Around 1900 many working-class Amsterdammers lived in cramped quarters, often without running water or electricity and heated with peat. The **Woningwet** (Housing Act) was passed in June 1901 and came into force on 1 August 1902. It did three things that mattered here:

- Cities could declare dwellings unfit to live in and clear slums. They also had to adopt building regulations and draw up extension plans.
- Associations working only for public housing could be approved as *toegelaten instellingen* (approved institutions) and borrow cheaply from the state through the city.
- Cities could build homes themselves.

The result was a new kind of client: not a speculative builder trying to cram in as many flats as possible, but a non-profit association that wanted good homes and was willing to pay an architect for them.

## The associations

Amsterdam had dozens of housing associations, and they were organised along the political and religious lines of Dutch society. Some of the names you will meet on this map:

- **Rochdale** (1903) was a cooperative named after the English cradle of the cooperative movement. It built the city's first Housing Act homes in 1909, in the Van Beuningenstraat, to designs by J.E. van der Pek. Van der Pek's Rochdale block of 1909–1912 in the Indische Buurt faces Berlage's [Berlageblokken](place:) across the Balistraat. The city register says Rochdale's [corner block on the Bellamystraat](place:Bellamystraat housing by La Croix), designed by G.F. la Croix in 1917, was the first Housing Act job given to an Amsterdam School architect.
- **Eigen Haard** ("Our Own Hearth", 1909) was a socialist association. It commissioned Michel de Klerk's [second block](place:Spaarndammerplantsoen, De Klerk's second block) on the Spaarndammerplantsoen and then [Het Schip](place:), with 102 flats, a post office and a school. In Noord it hired Jan Gratama, the man who coined the name "Amsterdam School", for [low-rise blocks in the Vogelbuurt](place:Gratama's blocks, Kievitstraat and Spechtstraat).
- **Het Westen** was another socialist association in the Spaarndammerbuurt. Its Zaanhof (Herman Walenkamp, designed 1916) is a double ring of housing around a green court. It is rationalist rather than Amsterdam School, so it is not on this map.
- **De Dageraad** ("The Dawn") was a socialist association too. In 1920 it gave De Klerk and Piet Kramer the commission for [De Dageraad](place:) in Plan Zuid: 294 homes and six shops on a site first meant for a hospital.
- **Algemeene Woningbouwvereniging** (founded 1910, today part of Stadgenoot) built most of the Diamantbuurt to designs by J.C. van Epen: see [Diamantbuurt housing (Van Epen)](place:).
- **De Samenwerking** was a cooperative that built for the middle class. Its estates include Van Epen's garden court [Harmoniehof](place:) and Staal's [block on the Bronckhorststraat](place:Staal block Bronckhorststraat).
- **Amstels Bouwvereniging** was a different animal: a cooperative of 71 building firms. It took land on leasehold in 1921 and by 1925 had built 1,739 small flats in the Rivierenbuurt. Sixteen of the firms went bankrupt along the way. Its architects included Margaret Staal-Kropholler, the first woman to practise as an architect in the Netherlands, on the Holendrechtstraat, and G.J. Rutgers at the Rijnstraat. See [Holendrechtstraat (Margaret Staal-Kropholler)](place:) and [Rijnstraat gateway (Rutgers)](place:).

Religious associations built in the same style. The Protestant Patrimonium commissioned the [Polanenstraat block](place:Polanenstraat block and Polanentheater), with a Calvinist school at its heart. The Jewish Handwerkers Vriendenkring built the [Uilenburg housing block (Jo Mulder)](place:).

## The city: Wibaut, De Miranda and the Woningdienst

Floor Wibaut became the first socialist (SDAP) alderman in 1914. He held office until 1931, with two short breaks, and was responsible for public housing, labour and, from the First World War, food distribution. He sponsored a proposal for 2,000 workers' homes and pursued slum clearance after the war. In the 1920s Monne de Miranda took over day-to-day housing, while Wibaut concentrated on finding the money. De Miranda was alderman from 1919 to 1939, with breaks. He also opened public bathhouses, such as the [Badhuis Boerhaaveplein (Badhuistheater)](place:) in 1921 and the [Badhuis Diamantstraat](place:) in 1926, which the new blocks needed because their flats had no bathrooms. Wibaut unveiled the memorial to the socialist politician P.L. Tak on De Dageraad in 1924. In 1942 the German occupiers arrested De Miranda, and he died after mistreatment in Kamp Amersfoort that November.

The **Gemeentelijke Woningdienst** (Municipal Housing Department) was set up in 1915 under Arie Keppler, who ran it until 1937. It supervised the associations and built on its own account, from the [Kraaipanbuurt (Gratama & Versteeg)](place:) in Oost to the garden villages of Noord (see [Garden villages and Betondorp](doc:Garden villages and Betondorp)). Keppler favoured Amsterdam School architects such as De Klerk, Gratama and Boeyinga.

## Façades and floor plans

The Amsterdam School is often judged by its façades, and that is partly fair. The national register says of De Dageraad that its plans were subordinated to the effect of the outside. The critic Tim Brinkhof argues in *Jacobin* that in theory the movement gave tenants' material and spiritual needs equal weight, but "in practice, the latter often took priority."

Behind many façades there were in fact two offices. Zeger Gulden and Melle Geldmaker were housing specialists who drew dwelling plans for Berlage, Van der Mey and Staal. They also designed whole blocks of their own, such as the [Spaarndammercarré (Gulden & Geldmaker)](place:) next to De Klerk's first block and the [Pastelstraat blocks (Gulden & Geldmaker)](place:) for Rochdale and Amsterdam-Zuid.

In [Plan West](place:) (1925–1927) the division was total. The developer H. van der Schaar built some 6,000 homes, and Gulden & Geldmaker drew all of their floor plans in advance. Berlage, Kramer, Wijdeveld, Staal and the other invited architects only designed the street fronts, which a beauty committee then judged. Architects mocked the method as *schortjesarchitectuur*, "apron architecture": a decorative apron tied over a standard house. Compare [Kramer's Hoofdweg blocks](place:Hoofdweg housing by Piet Kramer) with [Wijdeveld's](place:Hoofdweg housing by Wijdeveld). Their faces differ, but the flats behind them come from the same drawing board. The private [Krugerhof](place:) in Oost split the work the same way: Westerman drew the façades and Dunnebier the plans. The socialist daily *Het Volk* found its flats cramped and its rents high next to municipal housing.

## Homes for single people

Housing law was written for families. Single people mostly rented rooms or lived in boarding houses. In 1914 a Miss Kruis set up the cooperative Het Nieuwe Huis to give "(well-to-do) bachelors" flats of their own. With De Samenwerking she built [Het Nieuwe Huis](place:) on the Roelof Hartplein (1927–1928), an apartment hotel by Barend van den Nieuwen Amstel. It had 188 flats, dining rooms, a reading room, shops, a post office and meals from the cooperative kitchen. Across the square, the Catholic association for the protection of girls opened [Huize Lydia](place:) in 1927, a home with 158 rooms for women and girls, designed by Jan Boterenbrood.
