Michel de Klerk built only a handful of buildings in a career of little more than ten years. Even so, many people see him as the heart of the Amsterdam School, the Dutch branch of Expressionist architecture in which brick walls ripple, bulge and climb. If you want to see what the style was for and how far it could go, start with his work.

## A childhood in the Jewish quarter

De Klerk was born in Amsterdam on 24 November 1884 into a poor Jewish family. His father was a diamond cutter, and Dutch sources say Michel was his seventeenth child, born when the father was already 78. His father died in 1887, and the boy grew up in poverty in the old Jewish quarter around the Waterlooplein. The magazine *The Low Countries* calls that neighbourhood "fertile ground" for the socialism that was emerging at the time. Friends knew him as Sam.

He had little schooling, but he could draw. His drawing got him into a trade school and, at fourteen, into the office of the architect Eduard Cuypers, a nephew of the Rijksmuseum architect Pierre Cuypers.

## Eduard Cuypers' office

De Klerk stayed with Cuypers for about twelve years, working his way up from office boy to architectural draughtsman. Cuypers was a conservative designer himself, but he gave his young staff plenty of freedom and let them use his large library. Two of his other employees were Jo van der Mey and Piet Kramer. English-language histories of the movement say that the Amsterdam School began in this office.

In 1910 De Klerk married Lea Jessurun, Cuypers' secretary. That year he also travelled through Scandinavia, and Scandinavian brick buildings left a lasting mark on his work. A postcard he sent from Copenhagen showing a tower is often linked to the tower of Het Schip.

## First buildings

De Klerk's first independent work in Amsterdam was the [Hillehuis](place:Hillehuis) on the Johannes Vermeerplein (1911–1912). It is a block of flats for the building contractor Klaas Hille, and De Klerk got the commission through the office of the architect Herman Baanders. The national register says its "angular and spiky detailing" announces the start of the Amsterdam School.

At the same time Van der Mey was designing the [Scheepvaarthuis](place:Scheepvaarthuis) (Shipping House) on the Prins Hendrikkade, and he brought in De Klerk and Kramer to help. The building is generally counted as the first fully Amsterdam School building. De Klerk designed one boardroom down to the last detail: the panelling, furniture, carpet, lamp and door.

## Three blocks in the Spaarndammerbuurt

The Spaarndammerbuurt in the west of the city is where the style first reached the street. De Klerk built three blocks there in a row:

1. **The first block (1913–1915).** It stands on the north side of the Spaarndammerplantsoen, a small park. Its client was not a housing association but the contractor Klaas Hille again, who built it as private rented housing for workers. The register calls it a closed, "castle-like" block, quite calm, but already treated as one sculpted mass with brick used as a decorative skin. See [the first block](place:Spaarndammerplantsoen, De Klerk's first block).
2. **The second block (1915–1916).** It faces the first block across the plantsoen and was his first block for the socialist housing association Eigen Haard (Own Hearth). Here red and yellow brick are used "clearly non-constructively", and the roofs make no structural sense. Dates in the sources differ: the register gives 1914, and the literature gives 1915–1916 for the design, with building finished around 1916–1918. See [the second block](place:Spaarndammerplantsoen, De Klerk's second block).
3. **[Het Schip](place:Het Schip) (1917–1920).** This was also for Eigen Haard. Once the second block had taken the south side of the plantsoen, the third block went to a leftover triangular plot by the railway. Its 102 flats had separate rooms, their own toilets and plenty of light. A small post office with a public telephone sat at the sharp end of the block, and an existing school was absorbed into it. The slender tower has no function at all. Today the block houses Museum Het Schip, the museum of the Amsterdam School.

The neighbouring architects had to fit in around De Klerk. Next to the first block, the city's aesthetics committee spent a long time debating the four corner blocks of the [Spaarndammercarré](place:Spaarndammercarré (Gulden & Geldmaker)) by Gulden & Geldmaker.

## De Dageraad and Plan Zuid

In 1920 the socialist housing association De Dageraad (The Dawn) commissioned De Klerk and Piet Kramer to design 294 homes in Berlage's Plan Zuid, the city's southern extension. In [De Dageraad](place:De Dageraad), De Klerk is credited with the façades around the two small squares and Kramer with much of the street walls. The register calls it the Amsterdam School "in its most lively, irrational phase". De Klerk saw the first homes completed shortly before he died.

Around the same time he designed the calmer [north side of the Vrijheidslaan](place:Vrijheidslaan north side (De Klerk)) (1921–1922), where long, flat brick walls already point to the more restrained phase of the style. According to the Dutch Wikipedia article, the size and politics of Plan Zuid limited his freedom, and he turned increasingly to private clients. In 1923 he received the commission for the De Bijenkorf department store in The Hague.

## Death

De Klerk died of pneumonia on 24 November 1923, his 39th birthday. His colleague K.P.C. de Bazel, who had said De Klerk was one of the few with "the gift of animating stone", died on his way to the funeral. Piet Kramer took over the Bijenkorf commission (see [Piet Kramer and the bridges of Amsterdam](doc:Piet Kramer and the bridges of Amsterdam)).

The family's story did not end there. During the German occupation, De Klerk's widow Lea was murdered in Auschwitz in 1942 and their younger son Edo in Sobibór. The elder son, Joost, survived the war.

## The draughtsman

De Klerk was first of all a draughtsman. Sources describe portraits, among them of Pierre Cuypers and the poet Henriette Roland Holst, as well as travel sketches, posters, letterheads, a 1923 postage stamp, furniture and clocks. He cared about how a design looked on paper far more than how it would be built. Dutch sources say this went against Berlage, who wanted the material to lead the design. De Klerk also criticised Berlage's recent buildings in print in 1916. He designed three covers for *Wendingen*, the movement's magazine, and after his death the magazine devoted five issues to his work.

## Influence

The Belgian architect Huib Hoste praised the first Spaarndammer block as "one life of form and colour". Hendrik Wijdeveld called De Klerk "the most childlike and powerful" of architects. Through Wendingen and foreign magazines his work became known well beyond the Netherlands. The Indonesian architect Liem Bwan Tjie worked for him and later carried the style to the Dutch East Indies.

After 1930, functionalism pushed his romantic architecture aside and his name faded. Interest returned from the 1960s through foreign publications, notably Suzanne Frank's research. Several of his buildings were listed as national monuments, and in 1997 the Netherlands Architecture Institute (NAi) held the first large exhibition of his work. His archive is now at the Nieuwe Instituut in Rotterdam. Museum Het Schip, in his own building, tells his story today.
