Most famous Amsterdam School buildings were commissioned by housing associations or private firms. But the city was also a builder. Between about 1915 and 1935 the municipal Public Works department (*Dienst der Publieke Werken*) designed schools, bathhouses, pumping stations, police posts, offices and even a urinal in the new style. Together they make up one of the largest groups of buildings on this map.

## A department full of architects

Inside Public Works, the Buildings section (*Afdeling Gebouwen*) did the design work. A.R. Hulshoff headed it from 1916 and became city architect in 1924, according to the city monument register. As a rule the department signed its buildings collectively, which is why so many places on this map say simply "Public Works". The individual designers are known mostly from archives and later research:

- **Arend Jan Westerman** worked there from 1914 to 1922. He designed the [Gemaal Stadwijck](place:) pumping station, the round bathhouse on the Boerhaaveplein ([Badhuis Boerhaaveplein (Badhuistheater)](place:)) and the Fourth Trade School, now [Het Sieraad](place:). He also designed the brick-clad railway bridge over the Spaarndammerstraat ([Spaarndammerspoorbrug](place:)), with sculpture by Krop. The pair of schools on the Pieter Lodewijk Takstraat ([Tweede Openbare Handelsschool (Berlage Lyceum)](place:)) is also attributed to him.
- **Nicolaas Lansdorp** worked for the city from 1919 to 1932 and became its main school architect. His work includes the [Europaschool (Hygiëaplein)](place:), the [Wingerdweg schools](place:), the [Vossius Gymnasium](place:), the [Dongeschool](place:) and, with Hulshoff, the [City hall extension (Prinsenhof)](place:).
- **Pieter Lucas Marnette** designed the [Harm Smeengeschool](place:) for skippers' children, the [Gemeentetram headquarters](place:) and the [Traffic police station (Overtoom)](place:). With **G.J. Rutgers** he designed the [Telephone exchange (Singel 340)](place:).
- **Piet Kramer** ran the bridges side of the department. He drew his bridges in the [Werf Uilenburg (Public Works yard)](place:), which has the words PUBLIEKE WERKEN in gilded letters over its door.
- **E.P. Messer** designed the [Wilhelmina Gasthuis surgical clinic](place:) (1932–1933), a late, businesslike example.

Hulshoff also brought in **Hildo Krop**. The register for the [Zocherstraat HBS](place:) describes Krop at that point, around 1917, as the city's *unofficial* sculptor, getting his first commissions from Hulshoff. English Wikipedia says he joined the department's staff in 1916. Krop's sculpture appears on schools, bridges, the tram offices and the telephone exchange (see [Hildo Krop and building sculpture](doc:Hildo Krop and building sculpture)).

## Schools: civic art for working-class children

The city register explains the thinking. Its text for the [Smaragdplein school](place:) describes the socialist belief that handsome schools would raise working-class taste. The Vossius register likewise says the social-democratic council treated school building as a matter "of the highest importance".

**In the old city** the schools had to fit into existing streets. Several Jordaan schools went up on the sites of cleared slums. At [Lindengracht 146 school](place:) (1927) only the street front was really designed, but its height and broad classroom windows still make it stand out from its neighbours. The [Theo Thijssenschool](place:) follows the department's "corner school" type: two corridors meet at a staircase in the angle and the classrooms face south. On Uilenburg, the clearance of the old Jewish quarter produced the [Double school Nieuwe Uilenburgerstraat](place:) (1920), two schools around one playground.

**In the new districts** there was room to experiment. The [Smaragdplein school](place:) (1920) was one of the first "double schools", with two schools under one roof sharing a gym. Nobody yet knew whether its surroundings would become a square or the inside of a block, so it was given good fronts on every side. In De Baarsjes the [Cabotstraat school island](place:) (1928) goes further. Three long schools stand side by side, and a high, curving brick wall screens their playgrounds from the street. The wall is architecture in its own right, and all four parts are national monuments.

**Art was part of the brief.** Krop's carvings at the Zocherstraat HBS were meant to have a moral, educational effect on pupils. At the [Wingerdweg schools](place:) in Noord he carved small fairy-tale figures for younger children. Inside the Handelsschool on the Pieter Lodewijk Takstraat are a seasons frieze by Joop Sjollema (1929) and a wall painting by Peter Alma on trade and transport (1930). The [Vijfde Montessorischool Watergraafsmeer](place:) has roof beams carved into animals by Krop and a Pied Piper by Jan Heijens. At the [Gemeentelijk Meisjeslyceum (Reijnier Vinkeleskade)](place:), Maria Hubrecht painted the geological eras, dinosaurs included.

## Bathhouses: a weekly bath for everyone

Many new working-class flats had a toilet but no bath. In autumn 1918 the city decided to build a series of public bathhouses (*badhuizen*) for these neighbourhoods. High wartime prices for materials delayed the plan.

![The round bathhouse on the Boerhaaveplein, opened in 1921](commons:File:Badhuis Boerhaaveplein 28.JPG)

The first was Westerman's [Badhuis Boerhaaveplein (Badhuistheater)](place:), opened on 6 July 1921. It was the city's first free-standing bathhouse, and its plan is a circle about nineteen metres across. Around a central waiting room were some twenty cubicles, and men and women had separate doors. The [Badhuis Diamantstraat](place:) of 1926 repeats the round plan. The national register calls it the third in a series of round municipal bathhouses. A stone by the door was placed by the tenants of the surrounding housing associations. Its design is attributed to Westerman, but he had left Public Works years before it was built, so the final design may be someone else's.

Other bathhouses took other forms. The [Former bathhouse Uilenburg (Splendor)](place:) (designed 1919) shared a building with a street-cleaning depot. The [Badhuis Meteorenweg](place:) (1927) in Tuindorp Oostzaan mixes the style with village forms. For comparison, the little farmhouse-shaped [Badhuis Kromme Distelstraat](place:) was designed by B.T. Boeyinga for the Municipal Housing Service, not for Public Works.

**Why they closed:** later rounds of urban renewal fitted showers into the homes themselves, and the bathhouses lost their purpose. Diamantstraat closed in the 1970s, Kromme Distelstraat in 1977 and Boerhaaveplein in the early 1980s. Several live on as a theatre, artists' studios, a music venue or homes.

## Utilities, police and offices

The department gave even technical buildings an architectural face.

- **Work and communication.** The [Arbeidsbeurs (Passeerdersgracht)](place:) (1915–1917) is the earliest purpose-built municipal labour exchange. Separate doors and floors sorted job seekers by trade and sex. The [Telephone exchange (Singel 340)](place:) (1916–1919) has brick piers whose capitals are carved as telephone poles with wires.
- **Police.** The small timber [Former police post, Buiksloterweg](place:) (1915–1916) faces the old centre across the IJ. The [Traffic police station (Overtoom)](place:) opened on 1 October 1924, a sign of how fast motor traffic was growing. Amsterdam's first traffic lights were reportedly operated from here around 1932.
- **Water.** The [Gemaal Stadwijck](place:) on the Amsteldijk still drains a polder into the Amstel, and its design was shown at the 1925 Paris decorative arts exhibition. Sources differ on its date: nl.wikipedia gives 1919, the city register 1921. [Rioolgemaal F](place:) (1924–1925) and [Gemaal Mercatorstraat](place:) (1927) pump sewage, the second under three Amsterdam crosses.
- **Power and trams.** The electricity company's substations ([Onderstation Borssenburgplein](place:), [GEB substation Eerste Helmersstraat](place:)) got Amsterdam School gables. The [Remise Lekstraat](place:) (1927–1930) could hold 320 trams and was then the largest tram depot in Europe.
- **The city itself.** The [City hall extension (Prinsenhof)](place:) (1923–1926) held a new council chamber and wedding hall. Across the canal stands the [Urinal on the Oudezijds Voorburgwal](place:) (1926), crowned by Krop's ranting orator, a joke aimed at the council chamber opposite.

By the 1930s budgets were tighter and tastes had changed. The later buildings, such as the WG clinic and the Vossius Gymnasium, are calmer and more businesslike, and in 1932 Lansdorp left the city.
