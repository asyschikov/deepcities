Walk anywhere in the canal ring, De Pijp or Amsterdam-Zuid and you will cross a Piet Kramer bridge, often without noticing. Look for brick piers set with blocks of granite, wrought-iron railings that curl at the ends, built-in benches, and now and then a stone creature peering at the traffic. Together these bridges are the largest body of Amsterdam School work in the city. In 2017 the bridge historian Sebastiaan Baggelaar told NL Times: "Nowhere else in the world has such a large collection of work of one architect."

## The architect

Pieter Lodewijk (Piet) Kramer (1881–1961) was born on the Prinsengracht, the son of a doctor. From 1903 he worked in Eduard Cuypers' office alongside Michel de Klerk and Jo van der Mey. English sources say he stayed until 1911; Dutch sources say 1913. With them he worked on the [Scheepvaarthuis](place:Scheepvaarthuis) (Shipping House), the building where the Amsterdam School began. With [Michel de Klerk](doc:Michel de Klerk (1884–1923)) he designed [De Dageraad](place:De Dageraad) (1920–1923), and after De Klerk's death in 1923 he was the leading architect of the movement.

In 1917 he joined the bridges section of the Dienst der Publieke Werken (the city's Public Works department), where he stayed until 1952. He made drawings for more than 500 bridges, and about 220 were built, 64 of them in the Amsterdamse Bos. He often designed the bridge-keepers' houses, the ironwork and even the planting as well. In the 1920s the section worked from the [Werf Uilenburg (Public Works yard)](place:), where, according to Dutch Wikipedia, Kramer designed his bridges.

## Bridges as street furniture

Before Kramer, bridge design at Public Works had been led by Jo van der Mey. His [Brug 283 (Waalseilandbrug)](place:) behind the Scheepvaarthuis (1913–1914), whose lamps are attributed to Kramer, and the [Hilletjesbrug (Brug 125)](place:) from his office are early examples. Kramer took the bridge further. For him a bridge was a piece of the street: a place to sit, a gateway into a neighbourhood, a stage for sculpture.

- The three Leidsestraat bridges of 1921, the [Koningssluis (Brug 29)](place:), [Brug 43 (Leidsestraat, Keizersgracht)](place:) and [Aalmoezeniersbrug (Brug 68)](place:), share one design.
- The [Leidsebrug (Brug 174)](place:) (1923–1925) was the first Amsterdam bridge designed with built-in benches.
- The [Lyceumbrug](place:Lyceumbrug) (1926–1928) is the city's largest flat bridge. It has bridge houses, and boat landings with changing rooms underneath, built at the request of the school's head.
- The four Raadhuisstraat bridges, [Brug 8 (Raadhuisstraat, Singel)](place:), [Warmoesbrug (Brug 22)](place:), [Niek Engelschmanbrug (Brug 106)](place:) and [Nieuwe-Wercksbrug (Brug 63)](place:), were designed in 1921–1923. Rising prices after the war delayed them until 1925. Together they make a fine walk from the Singel to the Westerkerk.
- The [Barbiersbrug](place:Barbiersbrug) has four diamond-shaped bridge houses that are still used as shops.

## Hildo Krop

Most of the sculpture is by Hildo Krop (1884–1970), a baker's son from Steenwijk who had trained in Paris, Amsterdam and Berlin. He carved on the Scheepvaarthuis in the workshop of Hendrik van den Eijnde, joined Public Works in 1916 and became the city sculptor. Krop designed the fabulous beasts of the Leidsestraat bridges in 1916; Johan Polet carved them. On the [P.L. Kramerbrug](place:P.L. Kramerbrug), the bridge later named after its designer, he added seals, shipworms and Gijsbrecht van Amstel. On the [Kinderbrug and Hildo Kropbrug (Muzenplein)](place:) he carved children with animals; Dutch sources say two of Kramer's daughters can be recognised among them. Other Krop bridges on this map include the [Boerenweteringbrug](place:Boerenweteringbrug), the [Joep Langebrug](place:Joep Langebrug) and the [Dick Schmüllbrug](place:Dick Schmüllbrug). Krop also worked with other designers, for example on the [Spaarndammerspoorbrug](place:Spaarndammerspoorbrug) by A.J. Westerman and on Berlage's [Berlagebrug and Schollenbrug (Brug 423, 340)](place:).

## The Vierwindstrekenbrug

The [Vierwindstrekenbrug](place:Vierwindstrekenbrug) over the Admiralengracht is one of the few where Kramer could, in Dutch Wikipedia's words, use his Amsterdam School style to the full, with brick, granite, wrought iron and seating. It was planned around 1931; the map gives 1932, and Krop's work is usually dated 1933. Only in 1996 did it get its name, after Krop's four figures of the "four winds" (compass points):

- for the North, an Inuit hunter with an igloo and two walruses
- for the South, an African warrior with spear and shield, a hut and two lions
- for the East, a Chinese dock worker with a pagoda, the Great Wall and two steamships
- for the West, an American businessman in a bowler hat, on the telephone, with an office building, share prices and two cars

These figures are ethnic stereotypes of their time. Three of the four reduce whole peoples to a single exotic type set among animals and huts, while the West alone stands for modern business. They show how Dutch people in the early 1930s, at the height of the colonial period, pictured the rest of the world. They are worth looking at for exactly that reason. Jan Trapman's tiger and polar bear by the steps to the Erasmuspark carry the same theme of faraway places.

## The Magere Brug: Kramer in the old style

The most famous bridge Kramer rebuilt looks nothing like his work. By the 1920s the wooden Magere Brug (Skinny Bridge) over the Amstel, built in 1871, was worn out. Kramer produced several designs in steel and stone, but the city council decided the new bridge should look as much as possible like the old one. The old bridge was taken down from 1929 and a slightly wider double drawbridge, designed by Kramer, opened in 1934. It has steel girders on concrete piles, hidden behind timber. Dutch Wikipedia notes that it lacks almost all of his usual features. The Magere Brug is not on this map, but it is a short walk from the Amstel bridges that are.

## From showpiece to plain

You can follow the style's whole life on Kramer's bridges:

- **1920s showpieces.** Brick, granite, ironwork and sculpture all come together. In Watergraafsmeer the [Kramer bridges on the Galileïplantsoen (Brug 235, 335, 336, 337, 338)](place:) (1928) have piers that look like stacks of draughts pieces. The [Machineslootbrug](place:Machineslootbrug) and [Molenslootbrug](place:Molenslootbrug) (1929–1930) have railings that end in sea anemones.
- **1930s.** The ornament thins out. Examples are the [Walenweeshuissluis (Brug 70)](place:) and [Dageraadsbrug (Brug 353)](place:) (1934–1935), and the bare [Pesthuysbrug](place:Pesthuysbrug) of 1938. Sculpture survives on prestige bridges such as [Brug 382 (Jan van Galenbrug)](place:), with Krop's warehouses, dockers and Jonah in the whale. The steel bascule of the [Mariniersbrug (Brug 272)](place:) (1935) sits on a bastion-like pier; the register credits Kramer, but other sources name Jan Leupen.
- **War and after.** The [Dick Schmüllbrug](place:Dick Schmüllbrug) was planned in 1931 and built during the German occupation. [Brug 427 (Wibautstraat)](place:) (1941) has brick balustrades instead of iron. The bridges at [Kramer's post-war bridges at Sportpark Drieburg (Brug 254, 267, 273)](place:) were designed around 1940 but built only in 1950–1951. The [Overtoomse Sluis](place:Overtoomse Sluis) (1948) still has a Krop sculpture. The [Omvalbrug (Brug 429)](place:) (1953) is one of his last.
- **Nieuw-West.** [Brug 604](place:Brug 604) and [Brug 605](place:Brug 605) (1953–1955) fit Kramer's brick into Cornelis van Eesteren's modernist garden cities. Brug 604 has sculpture by Jan Meefout rather than Krop.

## His buildings

Kramer was never only a bridge designer. In Amsterdam you can see:

- his first housing block, [Van der Helstplein (Kramer)](place:) (designed 1916)
- the dark [Huize de Windroos (Het Zwarte Huis)](place:) (1921–1922) and the [Kramer houses Heinzestraat](place:Kramer houses Heinzestraat)
- the [Coöperatiehof](place:Coöperatiehof), with its reading room and little bell tower
- the [Vrijheidslaan south side (Kramer, Marnette)](place:) in Plan Zuid
- in Plan West, the [Hoofdweg housing by Piet Kramer](place:Hoofdweg housing by Piet Kramer) and the [Paramaribostraat housing by Piet Kramer](place:Paramaribostraat housing by Piet Kramer)

Outside the map, his masterpiece is De Bijenkorf department store in The Hague (1924–1926), a commission he took over from De Klerk. English Wikipedia calls it the last example of "classic" Amsterdam School expressionism.

When Kramer died in 1961, the Rationalists were in charge, and no institution wanted his expressionist drawings and models. They were burnt. His bridges survive, and since the 2016 study by Sebastiaan Baggelaar and Pim van Schaik they have had renewed attention.
