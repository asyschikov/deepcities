Walk out of Amsterdam's old centre in almost any direction and you soon reach a ring of 1910s–1930s neighbourhoods built of brick: housing blocks with rounded corners, little towers that serve no purpose, windows divided like ladders, and bridges guarded by stone animals. This is the **Amsterdam School** (in Dutch *Amsterdamse School*), a Dutch branch of expressionist architecture that flourished from about 1911 to 1940. English sources usually date it 1910–1930. This map stretches the window a little at both ends to include the forerunners and the long tail.

![Het Schip, Michel de Klerk's housing block for Eigen Haard (1917–1920)](commons:File:Amsterdam Het Schip 006.JPG)

## Where it came from

The style grew out of one drawing office. Michel de Klerk (1884–1923), Piet Kramer (1881–1961) and Jo van der Mey (1878–1949) all worked for the architect Eduard Cuypers in Amsterdam until about 1910. They were reacting against the revival styles of the 19th century, but also against the sober, rational brick of H.P. Berlage, whose Stock Exchange had made "honest" brick respectable. Berlage's housing for the poor, such as the [Berlageblokken](place:) in Oost (1911–1915), and his plans for new districts gave the younger architects their framework. They filled it with something much wilder.

The starting point is usually given as the [Scheepvaarthuis](place:) (1912–1916), the shipping companies' office on the Prins Hendrikkade. Van der Mey designed it and brought in De Klerk and Kramer, and a team of sculptors, glass artists and smiths covered it in sea gods, ships and waves. The national register calls it the first building realised entirely in the Amsterdam School style. Van der Mey also designed [Brug 283](place:Brug 283 (Waalseilandbrug)) just behind it, one of the first bridges in the style. De Klerk's [Hillehuis](place:) (1911–1912), a block for the builder Klaas Hille, already shows the angular, spiky detailing that announced what was coming.

## The name

The architects never called themselves a school. The term came from the architect and critic Jan Gratama, who used it in 1916 in an essay in the volume published for Berlage's sixtieth birthday. He wrote of "the newest movement in building art: the modern Amsterdam School, with its expressionism, its modern romanticism, and its fantasy". In the spelling of the time that was the *Amsterdamsche School*. The Dutch spelling reform introduced in 1947 dropped the old *-sch* ending, which is why it is now written *Amsterdamse School*. In English it is simply the Amsterdam School.

## Wendingen and its rivals

In January 1918 the architects' society Architectura et Amicitia launched the monthly *Wendingen* ("turnings"). Its editor was Hendrik Wijdeveld, an architect and typographer. With its square format, Japanese-style binding and bold covers, it became the movement's showcase, with issues on De Klerk, Frank Lloyd Wright and much else. Sources disagree on when it ended: the last issues are dated 1931 or 1932.

The movement had enemies from the start. In 1917 the new De Stijl group rejected this kind of "modern baroque". In 1927 the young functionalists of De 8 published their own manifesto, and the Nieuwe Bouwen (Dutch modernism) they championed came to see the Amsterdam School as a costly detour. Modernists mocked its façades as *schortjesarchitectuur*, "apron architecture": decoration tied on in front of floor plans that someone else had drawn. Historians have since softened the contrast. Both movements grew from the same turn-of-the-century reform of Dutch art, but De Stijl looked for universal rules while the Amsterdam School trusted the individual artist.

## The city as client

The style spread because of a law and a city council. The Housing Act (*Woningwet*), passed in 1901 and in force from 1902, let the government lend money to non-profit housing associations. It also obliged towns to adopt building regulations and plan their expansion. In Amsterdam, socialist housing associations such as Eigen Haard and De Dageraad became the great patrons of the style. De Klerk's three blocks on the Spaarndammerplantsoen ([first block](place:Spaarndammerplantsoen, De Klerk's first block), [second block](place:Spaarndammerplantsoen, De Klerk's second block), [Het Schip](place:)) and [De Dageraad](place:) in Plan Zuid turned social housing into "workers' palaces".

The city itself built in the style too. The Public Works department (*Dienst der Publieke Werken*) designed schools, bathhouses, pumping stations and fire stations, usually without naming the individual architect. Its schools, such as the [Smaragdplein school](place:) and [Het Sieraad](place:), reflected the socialist belief that handsome buildings would raise working-class taste. From 1917 Kramer worked in its bridges section, where he drew hundreds of designs; about 220 were built, most with sculpture by Hildo Krop, such as the [P.L. Kramerbrug](place:).

In the big expansion plans many builders were private contractors who wanted standard flats. To keep up quality, the city put façades before an aesthetics or "beauty" committee (*schoonheidscommissie*). Jan Gratama was a leading member, and the committee favoured Amsterdam School architects. In [Plan West](place:) (1922) one firm drew all the floor plans and well-known architects designed only the fronts. That explains the long unified street walls of the [Hoofdweg](place:Hoofdweg housing by Wijdeveld) and Berlage's [Mercatorplein](place:).

## Phases

Each place on this map has a *phase*, a rough label, not a strict rule:

- **Precursor** (before about 1915): Berlage's rationalism and the first experiments, such as the [Hillehuis](place:) and De Klerk's [first block](place:Spaarndammerplantsoen, De Klerk's first block) on the Spaarndammerplantsoen.
- **Expressive** (about 1915–1923): the peak. It brought undulating walls, fantastic towers and lavish sculpture: [Het Schip](place:) and [De Dageraad](place:), which the national register describes as the style "in its most lively, irrational phase".
- **Sober** (about 1923–1940): the *verstrakte* ("tightened") phase, with flatter walls, cubic volumes and less sculpture. Examples are the Plan West blocks and, at the far end, the [Heineken cold store](place:Heineken brewery cold store (Heineken Experience)) of 1933–1934.
- **Late**: 1930s works that mix the style with the Nieuwe Bouwen or Frank Lloyd Wright, such as Staal's [Wolkenkrabber](place:12-verdiepingenhuis (De Wolkenkrabber)), plus post-war echoes such as Kramer's [Brug 604](place:) of 1953–1955.

The national register uses its own numbering. It places Het Schip in the "second phase" and De Klerk's [Vrijheidslaan blocks](place:Vrijheidslaan north side (De Klerk)) at the transition to the "third".

## De Klerk's death and the decline

On 24 November 1923 Michel de Klerk died of pneumonia, aged 39. Wendingen devoted a series of issues to his work, and Wijdeveld called him a "visionary". His death is usually taken as the end of the expressive peak. Budgets were cut, and from the mid-1920s builders favoured simpler, more businesslike versions of the style, with straighter lines and little or no sculpture. The Depression of the 1930s made expensive brick ornament look out of date, and the Nieuwe Bouwen took over the architectural debate. Even so, the style lived on in schools, bridges and housing well into the 1930s. In [Betondorp](place:Brink (Betondorp)) it was even cast in concrete, and Kramer kept designing bridges in it into the 1950s.
