The Amsterdam School was meant to be experienced as a whole, from the street plan down to the letterbox. Its architects worked closely with sculptors, smiths and glass artists, and treated a block of flats as one sculpted mass rather than a row of separate houses. On foot, though, it helps to know what to look for. This guide goes through the style's vocabulary from the ground up, with examples on the map.

## Brick: bonds, joints and colour

Brick is the starting point. Before about 1915 brick in workers' housing was purely structural. The Amsterdam School made it the main means of expression. New steel and concrete frames and the cavity wall turned the façade into a "skin" that could be shaped freely.

- **Bonds.** Look at how the bricks are laid. De Klerk used what he called *Noors verband*, really the northern Dutch "monk bond": two stretchers (long sides) and then one header (short end) in each course, with the headers lining up vertically. On [Het Schip](place:) the bond is part of the design. Staal's [Jan Evertsenstraat housing](place:Jan Evertsenstraat housing by J.F. Staal) and Roodenburgh's [Vespuccistraat housing](place:Vespuccistraat housing by Roodenburgh) use the same bond. At Van Anrooy's [Apollolaan 191](place:Apollolaan 191 (Van Anrooy)) the base switches to an asymmetric block bond.
- **Bricks on end.** Bricks are often set vertically (*staand verband*) to make a plinth, a band or a whole wall. The round wall of the [Boerhaaveplein bathhouse](place:Badhuis Boerhaaveplein (Badhuistheater)) is laid this way. Kramer's bridges, such as the [Ceintuurbrug](place:) and the [Judith Leysterbrug](place:), alternate horizontal and vertical courses.
- **Joints.** Deep or recessed joints throw a shadow line under each course, so a wall reads as horizontal bands. The register notes "deep joints" on the [Atlanta](place:Atlanta (Stadhouderskade 5-6)) office building and deeply raked joints on [Drie Koningen](place:Drie Koningen (Singel 200-208)).
- **Colour.** Het Schip mixes bright red and orange brick, and no two bricks are the same. Other blocks switch colour by storey.

## Walls that move: undulations and towers

The national register describes [De Dageraad](place:) as having "purely decorative undulations and jumps in the wall surfaces". Brick that seems to ripple is the style's signature. Look for:

- undulating balconies, as on Margaret Staal-Kropholler's [Holendrechtstraat](place:Holendrechtstraat (Margaret Staal-Kropholler)) block and the [Cabotstraat school island](place:);
- wavy parapets and panels, as on the [Harm Smeengeschool](place:), the [Jan Maijenschool](place:) and the stair towers of Kramer's [Hoofdweg housing](place:Hoofdweg housing by Piet Kramer);
- rounded corners where a block turns into a side street.

![Undulating balconies on the Holendrechtstraat (Margaret Staal-Kropholler, 1921–1922)](commons:File:Amsterdam Woonhuis Holendrechtstraat 1-47 011.JPG)

**Towers** mark corners, gateways and squares, often with no practical use. The one on Het Schip only marks the gate into the courtyard. Later towers are blockier: Berlage's pair on the [Mercatorplein](place:), the stepped corner towers of Van der Mey's [Jan Evertsenstraat housing](place:Jan Evertsenstraat housing by Van der Mey), and the corner towers of the [Krugerhof](place:).

## Windows

- **Ladder windows** (*ladderramen*). The best-known feature of all. Instead of tall panes, the glazing bars divide the window into wide, low panes stacked like the rungs of a ladder. They echo other horizontal lines such as window strips and deep joints. Het Schip alone has 64 window types in its front façades. The most common is a five-part ladder window. See also the purple-glass stair windows in Marnette's part of the [Vrijheidslaan south side](place:Vrijheidslaan south side (Kramer, Marnette)), and the corner bays of the [Jan de Liefdeschool](place:Jan de Liefdeschool (De Boomspijker)).
- **Stair-step windows** (*trapvensters*). Windows that climb with the stairs inside, giving a stepped or tall vertical strip on the façade. Look at the curved front of [Het Nieuwe Huis](place:) and the tall stair windows of the [Harmoniehof](place:).
- **Odd shapes.** Parabolas, trapezoids and pointed frames whose sides rise to a point. Look for the parabolic porches of the [Columbusplein school](place:Columbusplein school (Commer de Geus)) and the diamond glazing bars and wave-shaped lintels of the [Smaragdplein school](place:). Frames are thick and usually painted white against the dark brick.

## Roofs and roof tiles

The roof counts as much as the wall. Tiles are hung vertically on walls, wrapped around curves and used as pure ornament. On Het Schip orange and dark grey tiles cover roofs, wall strips and the tower, sometimes, as the restorers noted, with little thought for keeping out the rain. Look for the vertical tiles on Het Schip, the terracotta tiles in the top zone of De Klerk's [first block](place:Spaarndammerplantsoen, De Klerk's first block), the ridge tiles over the attic windows of the Holendrechtstraat, and the dark-red vertical tiles on the corner towers of the Krugerhof.

## Lettering, sculpture, iron, glass and doors

- **Lettering.** Names are built into the architecture, in angular, elongated letters. Examples are "GEBOUW-DE KOLK" in brass on [Gebouw De Kolk](place:), iron letters spelling 't ZONNEHUIS on the [Zonnehuis](place:), "LEESZAAL" on the library door on the [Brink in Betondorp](place:Brink (Betondorp)), and ZORGVLIED on the [Zorgvlied cemetery gates](place:). The [Talmud Tora school](place:Talmud Tora school (Tweede Boerhaavestraat)) spells its name in brick and Hebrew letters. Shops did the same, as on [Gunters & Meuser](place:).
- **Building sculpture.** Sculpture is carved into the building rather than stuck on it. Look for the sea gods and ships of the [Scheepvaarthuis](place:) and Hildo Krop's trade figures on [Het Sieraad](place:). Most of Kramer's bridges also carry Krop's work, such as the [Niek Engelschmanbrug](place:Niek Engelschmanbrug (Brug 106)) and the [Barbiersbrug](place:). Some of it reflects the colonial and ethnic stereotypes of its day. The four "winds" on the [Vierwindstrekenbrug](place:) show an Inuit, an African hunter, a Chinese stevedore and an American stockbroker.
- **Wrought iron.** Balustrades, gates, lamps and flagpole holders. Kramer's bridge railings are a study in themselves: the balustrades of the [Molenslootbrug](place:) end in sea anemones. Het Sieraad has grilles with wave motifs.
- **Stained glass.** Leaded glass often sits in fanlights and stairwells. Examples are Willem Bogtman's glass in the Scheepvaarthuis and the [Raw Aron Schuster Synagogue](place:), and the half-round fanlight of the [J.J. van Noortschool](place:).
- **Doors.** Doors get vertical ribs, ladder-pattern glazing and wavy bars. Look at the [Bellamystraat housing by La Croix](place:), the doors with a wavy vertical bar on Wijdeveld's [Amstelkade blocks](place:Amstelkade blocks (Wijdeveld)), and the double doors of Gebouw De Kolk.

## Expressive or sober?

In the **expressive** phase (about 1915–1923), walls ripple, roofs do no structural work, sculpture is everywhere and floor plans give way to the outside effect. Think of Het Schip and De Dageraad. In the **sober** or *verstrakte* ("tightened") phase that followed De Klerk's death in 1923, budgets shrank and the forms straightened. Buildings became compositions of cubic brick blocks with long horizontal window strips set against vertical towers, and the sculpture was sparing or gone.

Compare De Dageraad with De Klerk's [Vrijheidslaan blocks](place:Vrijheidslaan north side (De Klerk)) a few streets away: same years, same architect, but the long walls there are already calm. Compare also Kramer's richly carved Barbiersbrug (1927–1928) with the bare [Pesthuysbrug](place:) of 1938.

## Reading the registers' labels

Most places on this map were described by the national (RCE) or municipal monument registers, often decades ago. They use a vocabulary of their own:

- **Amsterdamse School** or *Amsterdamsche School*: the style itself, sometimes qualified by phase. The register calls Het Schip one of the most "irrational" highlights of the "second phase".
- **Zakelijk-expressionistisch** ("businesslike" or "matter-of-fact expressionist"): brick expressionism pared down to cubic volumes, close to the sober phase. The register uses it for the [Heineken cold store](place:Heineken brewery cold store (Heineken Experience)), the [Wilhelmina Gasthuis surgical clinic](place:) and the [Olympic Stadium](place:), among others.
- **Kubistisch-expressionistisch** ("cubist expressionist"): blocky, stacked volumes, as on the [Candida office building](place:).
- **Lineaire expressieve variant** ("linear expressive variant", a term of the city register): flat, closed fronts of strict geometry with little ornament, as on the [Public Works yard on Uilenburg](place:Werf Uilenburg (Public Works yard)).
- **Duits georiënteerd** ("German-oriented") brick expressionism: the register's term for the [Lloyd Hotel and KHL office](place:), closer to German models than to De Klerk.
- **Verstrakt** with **nieuw-zakelijke elementen**: sober Amsterdam School with touches of the Nieuwe Zakelijkheid (New Objectivity), as at Apollolaan 191.
- **Art deco**: decorative modern, as at the [Tuschinski Theatre](place:), which mixes it with Amsterdam School details.

These labels overlap and were never strictly defined. Read them as the register author's description of how ornate or pared down a building is, not as membership cards.
