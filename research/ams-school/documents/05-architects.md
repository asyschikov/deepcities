Michel de Klerk and Piet Kramer have documents of their own: [Michel de Klerk (1884–1923)](doc:Michel de Klerk (1884–1923)) and [Piet Kramer and the bridges of Amsterdam](doc:Piet Kramer and the bridges of Amsterdam). Most of the buildings on this map, though, are by other architects. Some were stars, some were city employees, and some were commercial practices turning out thousands of homes. Here they are in roughly the order they appear in the story.

## Jo van der Mey (1878–1949)

Joan Melchior van der Mey trained in Eduard Cuypers' office from 1898 alongside De Klerk and Kramer, and won the Prix de Rome in 1906. In 1911 he received the commission for the [Scheepvaarthuis](place:Scheepvaarthuis) (Shipping House), the building generally seen as the start of the movement. He brought in his former colleagues and a team of sculptors and craftsmen. As a designer at the city's Public Works department he also drew the [Brug 283 (Waalseilandbrug)](place:) and, from his office, the [Hilletjesbrug (Brug 125)](place:). His later housing is calmer: the [Atelierwoningen Jacob Obrechtstraat](place:) (1917), the [Jan Evertsenstraat housing by Van der Mey](place:) in Plan West, the [Merwedeplein west (Van der Mey)](place:) and the [Haarlemmermeerstraat blocks](place:).

## J.F. Staal (1879–1940) and Margaret Staal-Kropholler (1891–1966)

Jan Frederik Staal began in partnership with Alexander Kropholler, designing rationalist offices for the insurer De Utrecht. After they split in 1910 he moved towards the Amsterdam School. He sat on the editorial board of Wendingen from 1920 to 1930, and English Wikipedia says he was a lifelong socialist. His works on the map run from the [Emmaplein villas](place:) and the [Staal block Bronckhorststraat](place:) to the [Jan Evertsenstraat housing by J.F. Staal](place:), the [Telegraaf building](place:) and the twelve-storey tower of flats nicknamed the Wolkenkrabber (Skyscraper), [12-verdiepingenhuis (De Wolkenkrabber)](place:). By the end of his life he had moved on to the New Objectivity (Nieuwe Zakelijkheid).

Margaret Kropholler, Alexander's sister, trained in that same office and is described as the first woman in the Netherlands to practise as a professional architect. From 1916 she also worked independently. Her housing on the [Holendrechtstraat (Margaret Staal-Kropholler)](place:) (1921–1922), with its wavy balconies, was the first housing in Amsterdam designed by a woman. In 1925 she won a silver medal in Paris. She married Staal in 1936, and she wrote and lectured on how good design could lighten housework.

![Staal's Wolkenkrabber on the Victorieplein](commons:File:Wolkenkrabber voorzijde - Amsterdam - 20021366 - RCE.jpg)

## H.Th. Wijdeveld (1885–1987)

Hendrik Wijdeveld lived to 101. He was editor-in-chief of *Wendingen* (Turns), the square-format magazine of the society Architectura et Amicitia that showed the Amsterdam School to the world in 116 issues from 1918. Its covers and typography were as experimental as the buildings. His first Amsterdam building is the [Citroen jeweller's shop (Kalverstraat 1)](place:). In Plan Zuid he designed the [Amstelkade blocks (Wijdeveld)](place:); in Plan West, the [Hoofdweg housing by Wijdeveld](place:) and the matching [Wijdeveld's stadspoort (north block)](place:); and on the Nieuwmarkt, the façades of [Flesseman](place:Flesseman). His 1940 book *De Nieuwe Orde* (The New Order) hurt his reputation after the war, but he was rehabilitated and taught in the United States at Frank Lloyd Wright's invitation.

## G.J. Rutgers (1877–1962)

Gerrit Jan Rutgers is often called the most productive architect of the movement, with about 7,000 homes. Until around 1920 he followed Berlage, and he worked for a time as a city architect: the [Telephone exchange (Singel 340)](place:) and probably the [Zocherstraat HBS](place:) date from that period. After that he built all over the city:

- **In the centre:** the [Carlton Hotel (Grand Hotel Centraal)](place:) and [Herengracht 513-515 / Vijzelstraat 22-28](place:).
- **Plan Zuid:** the gateways at the [Rijnstraat gateway (Rutgers)](place:) and [Scheldestraat gateway (Rutgers)](place:), and the [Boerenwetering villas (Rutgers)](place:).
- **Oud-Zuid:** the [Koninginneweg 8 (Rutgers flats)](place:), the [Rutgers buildings at Valeriusplein](place:), the [Rutgers town houses Hacquartstraat](place:), the [Rutgers block Apollolaan 175–185](place:), the [Minervalaan II blocks (Rutgers)](place:), the [Rutgers houses Stadionweg / Gerrit van der Veenstraat](place:) and the [Lutherkapel](place:).
- **Plan West:** the [Hoofdweg housing by G.J. Rutgers](place:).

## Jan Gratama (1877–1947)

Jan Gratama was a critic and organiser as much as a designer, and in 1916 he was the first to use the name "Amsterdamsche School". He sat on the city's aesthetics committees, planned [Plan West](place:Plan West) with Gerrit Versteeg and A.R. Hulshoff, and designed the [Olympia (HAGA blocks)](place:) by the Olympic Stadium, which drew criticism because he also supervised the district for the city. Other works on the map are [Gratama's blocks, Kievitstraat and Spechtstraat](place:), the [Kraaipanbuurt (Gratama & Versteeg)](place:), the plan around the [Brink (Betondorp)](place:) and the [Incassobank archive building](place:). His story ends darkly. He became an alderman under the German occupation in 1943, and Dutch sources note that this made him share responsibility for the deportation of Jewish residents from homes he had designed himself. He was arrested after the war and died isolated.

## Cornelis Kruyswijk (1884–1935)

Cornelis Kruyswijk learned the trade at evening school and in the offices of Harry Elte and F.A. Warners. After the 1920 Primary Education Act gave religious schools equal funding, he built fourteen Christian schools in Amsterdam, among them ['t Smallepad school](place:'t Smallepad school), the [J.J. van Noortschool](place:J.J. van Noortschool) and the [Newtonschool (Cornelis Kruyswijk)](place:). His shop arcades frame the entrance to the Victorieplein: [Victorieplein shop galleries (Kruyswijk)](place:). Nearby is the [Korte Meerhuizenstraat block (Kruyswijk)](place:). He also designed churches on prominent sites, such as the [Willem de Zwijgerkerk](place:Willem de Zwijgerkerk), the [Apostolisch Genootschap Watergraafsmeer](place:) and [Clematisstraat 25 (Apostolisch Genootschap)](place:), and the shopfront at [Vijzelstraat 81 (Warmolts pharmacy)](place:).

## A.J. Westerman (1884–1966)

From 1914 to 1922 Arend Jan Westerman worked at Public Works, where city buildings were designed in a style close to the Amsterdam School. From those years come the [Gemaal Stadwijck](place:Gemaal Stadwijck) pumping station, the bathhouses at [Badhuis Boerhaaveplein (Badhuistheater)](place:) and [Badhuis Diamantstraat](place:), the [Tweede Openbare Handelsschool (Berlage Lyceum)](place:), [Het Sieraad](place:Het Sieraad) and the [Spaarndammerspoorbrug](place:Spaarndammerspoorbrug). He probably also designed the [Public Works school Batjanstraat](place:). Afterwards he built mainly for private developers: [Herengracht 613](place:Herengracht 613), the façades of [Krugerhof](place:Krugerhof) and part of the [Voltaplein](place:Voltaplein).

## Dick Greiner (1891–1964)

Dick Greiner was a carpenter's son who was largely self-taught. He worked for Cuypers, Warners, Gratama and Rutgers before setting up on his own in 1920. He is best known for Betondorp, the "concrete village" in Watergraafsmeer, and its square, the [Brink (Betondorp)](place:). Dutch sources argue that in hindsight he had more in common with the Nieuwe Bouwen (Dutch modernism) than with the Amsterdam School. On the map: [Rozengracht 229-233](place:Rozengracht 229-233), the [Rijnstraat 24-58 and IJselstraat (Boterenbrood, Greiner)](place:) and the late [Zonnehof (Greiner)](place:).

## Jordanus Roodenburgh (1886–1972)

"Daan" Roodenburgh spent about ten years with K.P.C. de Bazel and the Baanders brothers before starting his own practice in 1916. His work favours yellow brick and simple forms. He built the [Vespuccistraat housing by Roodenburgh](place:), the arcaded [Surinameplein (Roodenburgh blocks)](place:), part of the [Haarlemmermeerstraat blocks](place:), the [Vrijburg (Remonstrant church)](place:) and the structure behind Wijdeveld's [Flesseman](place:Flesseman). As a football official he also designed Ajax's wooden stadium (1915) and the De Meer stadium.

## F.A. Warners (1888–1952)

F.A. Warners, a contractor's son with "a strong eye on America", introduced the apartment building to Amsterdam. Dutch Wikipedia gives his first name as Philip; the heritage register writes Frederik Adriaan. His 1914 *etagehuis* (flat building) on the De Lairessestraat put every room of a home on one floor, which was new for the Netherlands. On the map:

- the flats [Westhove](place:Westhove) and the [Warners block Olympiaplein](place:)
- the cubic [Warners villas J.J. Viottastraat](place:) and the [Dobbelsteen houses (Hacquartstraat)](place:)
- two early office towers, [Atlanta (Stadhouderskade 5-6)](place:) and the ten-storey [Candida office building](place:)

## The Baanders brothers

Herman (H.A.J., 1876–1953) and Jan Baanders (1884–1966) ran one of the largest practices in the city, which was also a training ground for the movement. Michel de Klerk, a school friend of Jan's, worked there, as did Roodenburgh, C.J. Blaauw and others. Through Herman, De Klerk got the commission for the [Hillehuis](place:Hillehuis). The brothers designed the [Amsterdams Lyceum](place:Amsterdams Lyceum), [De Friesche (Rokin 115)](place:), the [Van den Brul clothing store (Nieuwendijk)](place:), houses among the [Apollolaan villas](place:Apollolaan villas), the [Rooseveltlaan and Maasstraat (Baanders)](place:) and part of the [Haarlemmermeerstraat blocks](place:). Their earlier design underlies the [Incassobank archive building](place:).

## Gulden & Geldmaker

Zeeger Gulden (1875–1960), a socialist (SDAP) member of the city council, and Melle Geldmaker (1874–1930), a former carpenter, were Amsterdam's housing specialists. A Dutch source estimates they designed some 15,000 homes in the city. Their [Spaarndammercarré (Gulden & Geldmaker)](place:) had to fit in next to De Klerk's first block. In Plan Zuid they built the [Pastelstraat blocks (Gulden & Geldmaker)](place:). In Plan West they drew the floor plans behind other architects' façades, such as the [Hoofdweg housing by Piet Kramer](place:) and the [Hoofdweg housing by Wijdeveld](place:). This split gave rise to the joke *schortjesarchitectuur* ("apron architecture"): famous names designed only the front.

## Harry Elte (1880–1944) and Jacob S. Baars (1886–1956)

Both men were the leading architects of Amsterdam's Jewish institutions between the wars. Harry Elte grew up in the Jewish quarter and was Berlage's assistant for ten years. His work is cooler and more geometric, influenced by Wright and Dudok. On the map are the [Villa Klatte](place:Villa Klatte), the [Raw Aron Schuster Synagogue](place:Raw Aron Schuster Synagogue) (1927–1928) on the Jacob Obrechtplein, his main work, and his own home at [Stadionweg 44 (Harry Elte's house)](place:). Under the occupation he was barred from working. He was deported via Westerbork to Theresienstadt, where he died in 1944; because of the persecution, none of his archive survives.

Jacob S. Baars designed the [Talmud Tora school (Tweede Boerhaavestraat)](place:) (1922–1925) for poor Jewish children who had moved with their families from the old centre to the Weesperzijde. The German occupiers closed the school in 1943. Baars' synagogue on the Linnaeusstraat was demolished in 1962.
