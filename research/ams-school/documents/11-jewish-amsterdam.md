In 1940 Amsterdam had an estimated 75,000 to 80,000 Jewish residents, just under one in ten of the city's people. Many of them lived not in the old Jewish quarter around the Waterlooplein but in the new districts of the 1920s and 1930s, in the brick blocks this map is about. This document follows that move, the Jewish architects and institutions of those districts, and what happened to their residents during the German occupation.

## Leaving the old quarter: Uilenburg

At the end of the 18th century more than 90 per cent of Amsterdam's Jews lived on four islands east of the old centre: Vlooienburg, Uilenburg, Valkenburg and Rapenburg. Many of them were poor. By the late 19th century Uilenburg was badly overcrowded, with alleys, back-houses and frequent disease. In 1910, 368 of its 861 homes were declared unfit to live in.

Uilenburg became the city's first slum-clearance area. In the 1920s, under the socialist alderman Floor Wibaut, the narrow streets were demolished and the broad Nieuwe Uilenburgerstraat was laid out. The city monument register calls the result a rare concentration of Amsterdam School architecture in the old centre:

- the [Uilenburg housing block (Jo Mulder)](place:) of 1926, whose client according to the register was the Jewish building fund Handwerkers Vriendenkring;
- the [Double school Nieuwe Uilenburgerstraat](place:) (1920);
- the [Former bathhouse Uilenburg (Splendor)](place:), for a neighbourhood where few homes had a bath;
- the [Werf Uilenburg (Public Works yard)](place:), next to the Uilenburger Synagogue of 1766 and the caretaker's house of a former nursery school for poor Jewish children.

## East: the Transvaalbuurt

Only part of the old population could return to the new homes. Many Jewish families moved east, to the Transvaalbuurt, laid out from 1903 to a plan by H.P. Berlage. The Handwerkers Vriendenkring, an association of mostly Jewish workers founded in 1869, set up a building fund in 1912 and built large housing blocks there. Next to them the city's own Housing Department built the ring blocks of the [Kraaipanbuurt (Gratama & Versteeg)](place:) (c. 1920–1924).

Jewish institutions followed. The [Talmud Tora school (Tweede Boerhaavestraat)](place:) (1922–1925), in the neighbouring Oosterparkbuurt, was a religious school for poor children, run by the Ashkenazi community. It moved here from rooms under the Uilenburger synagogue as its families moved out of the old centre. Its name is still spelled out in Hebrew letters on the corner. On the Linnaeusstraat, at the corner of the Polderweg, a new synagogue was consecrated in 1928, designed in Amsterdam School style by the same architect as the school. It was demolished in 1962, and nothing of it remains to visit.

## South: the Rivierenbuurt and the Jacob Obrechtplein

Better-off Jewish families moved south into Plan Zuid. From the 1930s many middle-class Jews settled in the Rivierenbuurt, and for them it was a step up from the old quarter. After 1933 they were joined by Jewish refugees from Germany. By 1940 about 17,000 Jewish people lived in the Rivierenbuurt.

![The Raw Aron Schuster Synagogue on the Jacob Obrechtplein, by Harry Elte (1928)](commons:File:Amsterdamrawaronschustersynagoge.jpg)

Further west, in Oud-Zuid, the community built the synagogue on the Jacob Obrechtplein, now the [Raw Aron Schuster Synagogue](place:). It was consecrated on 18 May 1928. The exterior follows Dudok and Frank Lloyd Wright, the interior the Amsterdam School, with stained glass from Willem Bogtman's studio. After the war it became the centre of religious Jewish life in Amsterdam. It carries the name of Aron Schuster, rabbi from 1942 to 1973, and it is still in use.

## Jewish architects

- **Harry Elte** (1880–1944) grew up in an Orthodox family in the old Jewish quarter and trained with Berlage. From 1925 he was architect to the Ashkenazi community (the Nederlandsch-Israëlietische Hoofdsynagoge), and he designed synagogues across the country. On this map are the Obrecht synagogue, [Villa Klatte](place:) (1918–1919, with G.F. Mastenbroek) and his own house, [Stadionweg 44 (Harry Elte's house)](place:), where he lived from 1929 to 1942.
- **Jacob S. Baars** (1886–1956), also a follower of Berlage, designed several Jewish buildings in Amsterdam. Among them are the Talmud Tora school and the Linnaeusstraat synagogue.
- **Michel de Klerk** (1884–1923), the leading figure of the Amsterdam School, was born into a Jewish family in the old quarter, the youngest of many children of an elderly diamond cutter. He designed [Het Schip](place:) and, with Piet Kramer, [De Dageraad](place:). He died in 1923, long before the occupation.

Jewish clients also commissioned private work. At [De Lairessestraat 89–107](place:), a Jewish couple, the Drilsma-Maykels, had their house fitted with a rare system of folding glass walls.

## The Merwedeplein and the Frank family

The Merwedeplein is a triangular green square in the Rivierenbuurt, framed by the [Merwedeplein west (Van der Mey)](place:) blocks and by J.F. Staal's lower blocks beside his tower, the [12-verdiepingenhuis (De Wolkenkrabber)](place:). In December 1933 Otto and Edith Frank, refugees from Frankfurt, registered at Merwedeplein 37-II. Their daughters Margot and Anne joined them by February 1934. The family lived there until 6 July 1942, when they went into hiding in the annex on the Prinsengracht. The flat has been restored and now houses writers who cannot work freely in their own countries. A statue of Anne stands on the square.

## Persecution and deportation

From 1941 the occupiers forced Jews out of public life. In the Rivierenbuurt a playground on the Gaaspstraat was closed to Jewish children and, from 3 November 1941, turned into a market where Jews had to shop. Harry Elte was barred from working for non-Jewish clients from May 1941. The Handwerkers Vriendenkring was dissolved in 1942, when all Jewish organisations were banned. The Talmud Tora school was closed in 1943.

In 1943 the raids came. On 20 June 1943 a large raid swept the Rivierenbuurt and parts of Zuid and Oost. In the Transvaalbuurt, where in the early 1940s most residents were Jewish, the last Jewish residents were rounded up that year and deported. Historian Bianca Stigter has called the neighbourhood a trap in which Jews were first isolated and then taken away.

The people connected with these buildings shared that fate. Harry Elte was held in Westerbork from September 1942. He was deported to Theresienstadt on 25 February 1944 and died there on 1 April 1944. Alfred Kohn, who with Ernst Cahn ran the Koco ice-cream parlour on the Van Woustraat, a base for a resistance group, was killed in Auschwitz. Cahn was executed by firing squad. Bridge 401 nearby now bears their names: the [Ernst Cahn en Alfred Kohnbrug](place:). Of the roughly 17,000 Jewish residents of the Rivierenbuurt, about 13,000 did not survive the war. Across the city, sources put the share of Amsterdam's Jews who were murdered at around 75 to 80 per cent.

The buildings still stand. In front of Merwedeplein 37 lie *Stolpersteine*, small brass stones in the pavement, for the Frank family. The Joods Monument lists the victims of the persecution by name and by their last address.
