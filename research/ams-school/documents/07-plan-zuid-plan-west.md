The Amsterdam School got its city, not just its buildings, from two extension plans of the 1910s and 1920s: H.P. Berlage's Plan Zuid in the south and Plan West beyond the Kostverlorenvaart. Together with the later districts they form the *Gordel '20-'40*, the ring of interwar neighbourhoods around the 19th-century city. Berlage drew the streets, and the younger architects of the Amsterdam School filled them in.

## Berlage's Plan Zuid

Amsterdam asked Berlage to plan the empty polder between the Amstel and the Schinkel around 1900. His first plan of 1904 (approved in 1905) had picturesque, winding streets. Critics complained that it did little for affordable housing, and research showed it would be very costly and inefficient to build. The First World War delayed it further.

Berlage revised it in 1914–1915. The new Plan Zuid had straight, fairly narrow streets and long housing blocks, cut through by a few broad, monumental axes, with squares where they met. The city council approved it in 1917. It covers today's Rivierenbuurt, Apollobuurt, Stadionbuurt and part of the Nieuwe Pijp.

Berlage thought about the street as a room whose walls are façades. In lectures of 1914 he argued that individual architects, each in their own style, would never produce a coherent cityscape without firm regulation. Ideally a whole block would be designed and built at once.

## Controlling the façades

That idea needed people to enforce it. Plan Zuid was built largely by private developers and housing associations (see [Workers' palaces](doc:Workers' palaces: housing associations and the Housing Act)), and their designs went to the city's beauty committee (*Schoonheidscommissie*). For the south a dedicated Commissie Zuid was formed. It judged façades in detail, often sent them back, and handed architects outline silhouettes to work within. Sources differ on the date: a TU Delft study says 1925, another account "after 1926". On the [Minervalaan II blocks (Rutgers)](place:), Jacob Kaas carved portraits of committee members into the capitals of Rutgers' shopping arcades. Even the [Haarlemmermeerstraat blocks](place:) of 1930–1932, by three different firms, keep to a shared symmetry that the city register traces back to the Commissie Zuid.

Supervision was not always disinterested. Jan Gratama supervised the building of the Stadionbuurt for the city while he was a partner in the company that built [Olympia (HAGA blocks)](place:), and the professional press noticed.

## A walk through the Rivierenbuurt

The Rivierenbuurt shows Plan Zuid at its most complete. Start at [De Dageraad](place:) (1918–1923), built on a site Berlage had reserved for a hospital, whose street pattern it still follows. Then walk down the Vrijheidslaan, called the Amstellaan until 1946. The [Vrijheidslaan north side (De Klerk)](place:) and the [Vrijheidslaan south side (Kramer, Marnette)](place:) face each other as one composition. The avenue ends at the Victorieplein, where three main streets fork in a Y.

Berlage wanted towers at key points. Staal's [12-verdiepingenhuis (De Wolkenkrabber)](place:), finished in 1932, is the one that rose here. English Wikipedia calls it the only one of Berlage's planned towers to be built, while the city's description says it is one of the few. The building code allowed only four storeys of flats and had to be changed first. Hildo Krop's statue of Berlage stands in front of it.

From 1933 German-Jewish refugees settled in these new streets, among them the Frank family on the Merwedeplein. By 1940 some 17,000 of Amsterdam's Jews lived in the Rivierenbuurt. About 13,000 of them did not survive the war.

## The Amstelkanaal bridges

The Amstelkanaal was dug as part of Plan Zuid. It joins the Amstel to the Schinkel and separates De Pijp from the Rivierenbuurt. Five bridges cross it. Public Works engineers did the structures, Piet Kramer the brick, granite and ironwork, and Hildo Krop most of the sculpture:

1. [P.L. Kramerbrug](place:) (bridge 400, designed 1917, finished 1921) on the Amsteldijk, with Krop's seals, sea lions, shipworms and Gijsbrecht van Amstel.
2. [Ernst Cahn en Alfred Kohnbrug](place:) (401, 1927), plain granite wing walls and no sculpture. It is named after the owners of the ice-cream parlour Koco, whose resistance group was arrested in 1941.
3. The Han van Zomerenbrug (402), a footbridge by the [Tweede Openbare Handelsschool (Berlage Lyceum)](place:).
4. The Gerard Revebrug (403).
5. [Barbiersbrug](place:) (404, opened 1928), with four tiny shops built into its corners.

A sixth bridge, at the canal junction called De Kom, was never built. On the Zuider Amstelkanaal, the [Dick Schmüllbrug](place:) (designed 1931, built 1940–1942) carries Krop's "Ode to Berlage".

## Plan West

West of the Kostverlorenvaart lay the farmland of Sloten, which had drawn up its own extension plans in 1909 and 1916. When Amsterdam annexed Sloten on 1 January 1921, a committee of three took those plans over: the architects Jan Gratama and Gerrit Versteeg, and A.R. Hulshoff of Public Works. In 1922 they presented Plan West, a plan for 6,000 working-class homes. It kept the old ditch pattern and made the old Hoofdweg its spine, with the Mercatorplein halfway along as the district centre.

[Plan West](place:) followed Plan Zuid's idea of long street walls, some up to 250 metres. It was built very quickly, mainly in 1925–1927, by the contractor H. van der Schaar. One office drew all the floor plans, and the famous names designed only façades. Each architect usually got both sides of a street, so the Hoofdweg reads as a sequence of rooms. [Kramer](place:Hoofdweg housing by Piet Kramer) comes first, with towering corner blocks at the Postjesweg, then [Rutgers](place:Hoofdweg housing by G.J. Rutgers), then Berlage's [Mercatorplein](place:), then [Wijdeveld](place:Hoofdweg housing by Wijdeveld), whose paired towers form a city gate at the Jan van Galenstraat; see [Wijdeveld's stadspoort (north block)](place:). Side streets carry work by [Staal](place:Jan Evertsenstraat housing by J.F. Staal), [Van der Mey](place:Jan Evertsenstraat housing by Van der Mey) and [Roodenburgh](place:Vespuccistraat housing by Roodenburgh). Churches and Public Works schools, such as the [Jeruzalemkerk](place:) and the [Jan Maijenschool](place:), were slotted into the blocks. *Wendingen* devoted a double issue (1927, no. 6/7) to the plan.

The Mercatorplein is the one Amsterdam School square that Berlage designed in full, though again only its façades. He planned three towers and two were built. The north tower was demolished in 1961, and the whole north wall was rebuilt to the original design in 1995–1998.

## The Surinameplein, where the plans meet

The round Surinameplein is where the Hoofdweg begins and the original Plan West area ended. It sits on the seam between the districts of Zuid and De Baarsjes. Jordanus Roodenburgh designed its curved walls, the [Surinameplein (Roodenburgh blocks)](place:), in phases from 1927 to 1929, with corner towers and tall decorative walls. From the middle you look north up the Hoofdweg into Plan West, while the Surinamestraat and Sloterkade lead off towards Zuid. The square was only closed on all sides in 1955–1956.
