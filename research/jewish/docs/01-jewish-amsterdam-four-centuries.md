For more than four hundred years Amsterdam has had a Jewish community, and for most of that time it was the largest in Western Europe. Amsterdam Jews called their city *Mokum*, from the Hebrew word *makom*, "place": the place, the city where they could live. This map gathers what is left of that history in the streets, from Golden Age synagogues to a stumbling threshold at a tram stop. This document is the overview. Each section points to a longer document.

## Two nations in one city (1600 to 1795)

The first to come were Portuguese and Spanish *conversos*, families whose ancestors had been forced to become Christians on the Iberian Peninsula. From the 1590s merchants among them settled in Amsterdam, a booming port that offered trade and, under the Union of Utrecht, freedom of conscience. Many returned to the faith of their ancestors. A first congregation, Beth Jacob, was founded around 1602; two more followed, and in 1639 the three merged into Talmud Torah, which still exists. In 1614 the community bought land for a cemetery at [Ouderkerk aan de Amstel](place:Beth Haim, the Portuguese Jews' cemetery at Ouderkerk), and in 1675 it inaugurated the [Esnoga](place:Portuguese Synagogue (Esnoga)), then the largest synagogue in the world. Their story, including their part in the Atlantic colonial economy and in slavery, is told in [The Portuguese Nation](doc:The Portuguese Nation).

In the early decades of the seventeenth century Jews from the German lands followed, and after the pogroms of 1648 and 1649 refugees from Poland and further east. These Ashkenazim, the *Hoogduitsers* or High Germans, set up their own congregation in 1635 and consecrated the Great Synagogue in 1671, across the water from the Portuguese. They were mostly poor, and by the eighteenth century they far outnumbered the Portuguese: around 1795 Amsterdam had some 24,000 Jews, 20,000 of them Ashkenazim, about a tenth of the city's population. See [The Hoogduitsers](doc:The Hoogduitsers).

There was no ghetto, no wall and no gate. But Jews could not join the guilds, which kept them out of most crafts, and they lived close together on the islands of Vlooienburg, Marken and Uilenburg and along the Jodenbreestraat. That district, the Jodenbuurt, is the subject of [The Jodenbuurt and its clearance](doc:The Jodenbuurt and its clearance). Both communities ran their own poor relief, orphanages, schools and burial societies, because no one else would; see [Charity, schools and care](doc:Charity, schools and care).

## Citizens (1796 to 1940)

On 2 September 1796 the parliament of the Batavian Republic made Jews equal citizens, the second country in Europe after France to do so. Two years later two Amsterdam Jews sat in the national assembly. Under King William I the communities became a state-recognised denomination, and the government pressed hard for Dutch to replace Yiddish. In the nineteenth century Jews moved into the professions, politics and banking, and a new elite took shape: the lawyer Jonas Daniël Meijer, the banker A.C. Wertheim, the physician and city improver [Samuel Sarphati](doc:Samuel Sarphati). See [Emancipation and assimilation, 1796 to 1940](doc:Emancipation and assimilation, 1796 to 1940).

Most Amsterdam Jews stayed poor far longer. Many were street traders and market people; many worked in the diamond trade, which boomed after South African diamonds reached Amsterdam around 1870. In 1894 the diamond workers founded the ANDB under [Henri Polak](doc:Henri Polak), the union that won the eight-hour day. See [Diamonds and the ANDB](doc:Diamonds and the ANDB).

From the late nineteenth century families who could afford it left the old quarter for the Plantage, the Weesperbuurt, the new districts of Oost and, between the wars, Zuid, where a neighbourhood like the Rivierenbuurt grew from about 4,000 Jewish residents in 1930 to some 17,000 in 1940. New synagogues followed them, from the [Gerard Dou Synagogue](place:Gerard Dou Synagogue) of 1892 to the [Lekstraat synagogue](place:Lekstraat synagogue) of 1937: see [Synagogues of Amsterdam](doc:Synagogues of Amsterdam). Jewish Amsterdam also shaped the city's theatre, music, football, slang and food: see [Mokum: Jewish words and culture in Amsterdam](doc:Mokum: Jewish words and culture in Amsterdam). In the 1930s refugees from Germany arrived, among them the family of Anne Frank.

## Destruction (1940 to 1945)

When Germany invaded in May 1940, about 140,000 Jews lived in the Netherlands, and Amsterdam was home to roughly half of them. In 1941 more than 77,000 people in the city were registered as Jews under the occupier's rules, more than half of all Jews in the country. Step by step they were registered, marked, excluded from schools, parks, markets and work, robbed, and from July 1942 deported, mostly through the Hollandsche Schouwburg and Westerbork to Auschwitz and Sobibor. About three in four Amsterdam Jews did not survive; in the country as a whole some 102,000 Jews were murdered. The documents [Persecution, 1940 to 1943, and the Jewish Council](doc:Persecution, 1940 to 1943, and the Jewish Council), [February 1941](doc:February 1941), [The Hollandsche Schouwburg, the crèche and the children](doc:The Hollandsche Schouwburg, the crèche and the children) and [Hiding, help and resistance](doc:Hiding, help and resistance) tell that story address by address. Two lives from those years have their own documents: [Etty Hillesum](doc:Etty Hillesum) and [Walter Süskind and Henriëtte Pimentel](doc:Walter Süskind and Henriëtte Pimentel).

## After 1945

On 9 May 1945 about a thousand survivors gathered in the Portuguese Synagogue for the first service after the liberation. Many came back to find their homes taken and their families gone, and the old Jodenbuurt, stripped for firewood in the Hunger Winter, was largely pulled down in the decades that followed. See [Return and the postwar years](doc:Return and the postwar years). Remembrance came slowly and in stages, from the Dokwerker of 1952 to the [Holocaust Names Monument](place:National Holocaust Names Monument (Holocaust Namenmonument)) of 2021 and the stones in the pavement: see [Remembering: monuments, names and stones](doc:Remembering: monuments, names and stones) and [Stolpersteine](doc:Stolpersteine).

Jewish life went on, smaller and further south, in Buitenveldert and Amstelveen. The Ashkenazi, Portuguese and Liberal congregations and several smaller ones still hold services; the [Jewish Museum](place:Jewish Museum (Joods Museum), four synagogues in one), the Portuguese Synagogue and the National Holocaust Museum are part of the Jewish Cultural Quarter. See [Jewish Amsterdam today](doc:Jewish Amsterdam today).

## People

Six short biographies follow people whose Amsterdam lives are tied to places on this map: the philosopher [Spinoza](doc:Spinoza), born at the edge of [Vlooienburg](place:Vlooienburg under the Stopera, the plaque that took thirty years, and Spinoza); the rabbi and printer [Menasseh ben Israel](doc:Menasseh ben Israel); the union leader [Henri Polak](doc:Henri Polak); the city improver [Samuel Sarphati](doc:Samuel Sarphati); the diarist [Etty Hillesum](doc:Etty Hillesum); and [Walter Süskind and Henriëtte Pimentel](doc:Walter Süskind and Henriëtte Pimentel), who got children out of the deportation centre.

## How to use this map

Every place on the map has an era (before 1796, 1796 to 1940, 1940 to 1945, after 1945) and a theme (synagogues and religion, community and care, work and trade, culture and sport, persecution and war, remembrance, neighbourhoods, cemeteries). Use the filters to follow one thread through the city. The walks link places into routes on foot or by bike, from the old islands of the Jodenbuurt to the Rivierenbuurt and the cemeteries outside the city.

A few practical points. Synagogues in use are working buildings: most can only be seen from outside, or at services and on open days. The Portuguese Synagogue and the Jewish Museum are open to visitors on one ticket, with hours that change on Jewish holidays; some sites close on Saturdays. Many places on this map are private homes or schools, marked by a plaque, a stone or a name: look, but leave residents in peace.
