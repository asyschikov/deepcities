In the 1590s a new kind of migrant began to arrive in Amsterdam: merchants from Portugal and Spain whose families had been Christian, on paper, for generations. Their ancestors had been forced to convert at the end of the fourteenth and in the fifteenth century, and the Inquisition still suspected them of practising Judaism in secret. In Amsterdam many of these *conversos* or "New Christians" became "New Jews". They called themselves the *nação*, the Nation: the Portuguese Nation, as distinct from the Jews who came later from Germany and Poland.

## Why Amsterdam

The newcomers were drawn by trade as much as by safety. After the Dutch blockaded Antwerp, Amsterdam became the hub between northern and southern Europe, and Portuguese merchants brought their networks with them. The province of Zeeland gave Portuguese merchants the right to trade in 1587, the States General followed in 1588, and although Haarlem, Alkmaar and Rotterdam offered better terms, most chose Amsterdam. What they found there was not formal tolerance but room: Article XIII of the Union of Utrecht promised freedom of conscience, and slowly the city let them worship in private. There was never a ghetto.

A first congregation, Beth Jacob, was founded around 1602. Neve Salom and Beth Israel followed, and in 1639 the three merged into Kahal Kados de Talmud Torah, the congregation that still runs the Portuguese Synagogue. The Nation grew from about 2,000 people in 1650 to about 4,000 in 1700, most of them living on and around [Vlooienburg](place:Vlooienburg under the Stopera, the plaque that took thirty years, and Spinoza) and the Breestraat, which Amsterdam came to call the Jodenbreestraat. Their Christian neighbours included painters: [Rembrandt](place:Rembrandthuis (Rembrandt House Museum)) lived among them from 1639.

## A cemetery, a school and a dowry fund

Jewish graves may never be cleared, so a burial ground came first. The city would not allow one inside its walls, and in 1614 the community bought land at Ouderkerk aan de Amstel, against fierce local protest. [Beth Haim](place:Beth Haim, the Portuguese Jews' cemetery at Ouderkerk) is still the Portuguese community's cemetery; some 27,000 people are buried there, under stones carved in Portuguese, Spanish, Hebrew, Latin and Dutch, some with figures and scenes that few Jewish cemeteries elsewhere would allow. Funeral parties came by boat along the Amstel.

![Romeyn de Hooghe's etching of a funeral arriving by boat at the Portuguese cemetery in Ouderkerk, 1675](commons:File:Romeyn de Hooghe, Afb 010097005716.jpg)

The congregations ran a school together from 1616, Talmud Torah, organised in six classes by age, with a scholarship fund, Ets Haim, from 1637 that grew into the community's yeshiva. Former conversos had to learn Judaism from the beginning, and the school became known across the diaspora; its graduates led Sephardi congregations in Hamburg, London, New York and the colonies. The Ets Haim library, founded with it, is the oldest Jewish library still in use in the world. In 1615 wealthy members founded Dotar, a society that gave dowries to poor orphaned girls of the Nation, not only in Amsterdam but wherever they lived outside Spain and Portugal. Its lottery is still held every year around Purim.

## The Esnoga

For decades the Nation prayed in converted buildings, after the merger in a synagogue on the Houtgracht. In the 1670s, the high point of its wealth, it built something new: a free-standing synagogue on the Muidergracht, designed by the master builder Elias Bouman and inaugurated on 2 August 1675. The [Portuguese Synagogue, or Esnoga](place:Portuguese Synagogue (Esnoga)), holds 1,200 men and 440 women, has no electric light and no heating, and is lit by hundreds of candles in its brass chandeliers. Visitors at the time were astonished that a Jewish community could build so openly, and Portuguese families hung prints of it in their homes. It became the model for Sephardi synagogues in London, Willemstad on Curaçao, Paramaribo and New York.

![Emanuel de Witte's painting of the interior of the Portuguese Synagogue, around 1680](commons:File:Interieur van de Portugese synagoge te Amsterdam, SK-A-3738.jpg)

The Nation was also a place of intellectual conflict. Its leaders, keen to hold former conversos to rabbinic Judaism, punished dissent with the *cherem*, the ban; the most famous was pronounced on the young [Spinoza](doc:Spinoza) in 1656. Its rabbi [Menasseh ben Israel](doc:Menasseh ben Israel) printed books in Hebrew, Spanish and Latin for Jews and Christians alike. In 1665 and 1666 the community was swept up, like much of the Jewish world, in the excitement around the would-be messiah Sabbatai Zevi.

## Trade and wealth, poverty and exclusion

Portuguese Jews traded with Portugal and Spain, the Mediterranean and the Levant, ran sugar refineries, tobacco works and a silk industry, and dealt on the Amsterdam exchange. The period between 1648 and 1672 has been called the Golden Age of Portuguese Judaism in Amsterdam. Some families became very rich. The banking and trading family De Pinto, who had come from Lisbon by way of Antwerp, owned the house on the Sint Antoniesbreestraat now known as [Huis De Pinto](place:Huis De Pinto, the house that stopped a motorway); in 1696, during a riot of poor Amsterdammers against a new funeral tax, it was looted alongside the houses of burgomasters, a sign that the crowd equated wealth with Jews. A later member of the family, Isaac de Pinto (1717 to 1787), reformed the congregation's finances, wrote on trade, credit and national debt, and answered Voltaire's mockery of Jews in print.

Most members were not rich. The Nation took care of its own poor, and also tried to keep them few: from 1622 it paid poor Jews, its own and others, to move on, and in 1747 it drew up a plan to send a third of its poor to Suriname. In the late eighteenth century 55 per cent of Amsterdam's Portuguese Jews received charity. The community's [orphanage for boys](place:Aby Jetomim, the Portuguese boys' orphanage (Plantage Middenlaan 80)), its [old men's home](place:Nieuwe Herengracht 33, the stones of the Portuguese-Jewish old men's home) and its [almshouse for women](place:Fernandes Nuneshuis, a Portuguese-Jewish almshouse) are part of that story; see [Charity, schools and care](doc:Charity, schools and care). The Portuguese also kept their distance from the poorer Ashkenazim: see [The Hoogduitsers](doc:The Hoogduitsers).

## Brazil, Suriname, Curaçao: the Atlantic side

The Nation's history is also part of the history of Dutch colonialism and slavery. When the West India Company (WIC) held northern Brazil, from 1630 to 1654, Jews from Amsterdam settled there; in 1641 the Amsterdam rabbi Isaac Aboab da Fonseca went to Recife to lead Kahal Kados Zur Israel, the first Jewish congregation in the Americas. Jews in Dutch Brazil traded and owned many sugar mills, and sugar in Brazil was produced by enslaved Africans. When Portugal retook the colony in 1654, the Jews left, some back to Amsterdam, others to the Caribbean and to New Amsterdam, the later New York.

After Brazil, Dutch Sephardim spread across the Caribbean. From the 1690s Curaçao became the mother community of Caribbean Jewry, a trading post that supplied Spanish America. In Suriname, Dutch from 1667, Jewish planters settled along the Suriname River. Their settlements, first at Cassipora Creek from the 1650s and from the 1680s at Jodensavanne, were owned and governed by Jews and had privileges unmatched in the early modern Jewish world; they were inhabited, in UNESCO's words, by Jews "together with free and enslaved persons of African and Indigenous descent". Jodensavanne has been a World Heritage Site since 2023. By the eighteenth century Jews made up between a third and a half of the white population of Curaçao and Suriname. Some owned plantations worked by enslaved people, and, in the words of the historians Tirtsah Levie Bernfeld and Bart Wallet, the Jews of the islands "had a considerable stake in the slave trade".

The Amsterdam end was smaller than is sometimes claimed. Jews invested in the chartered companies, but modestly: of the first WIC's original capital of 3 million guilders, Jewish investors put in 36,000, or 1.2 per cent, and in 1656 seven of its 167 major shareholders were Jews. Historians have pointed out that such figures have been misused to blame Jews for the Atlantic slave trade, and also that the colonial side of Dutch Jewish history was long left out of both Jewish and Dutch history. Slavery reached Amsterdam homes too: notarial records show Portuguese families bringing enslaved African-born or African-descended servants from Brazil, whose status in a city where slavery had no legal basis was unclear. Some Black members of Portuguese households who had become Jewish were buried at Beth Haim.

## What remains

The Esnoga still holds services and is open to visitors, with the Ets Haim library beside it. Beth Haim can be visited at Ouderkerk. Vlooienburg lies under the Stopera, and Huis De Pinto survived the post-war demolitions of the Jodenbuurt by one council vote (see [The Jodenbuurt and its clearance](doc:The Jodenbuurt and its clearance)).
