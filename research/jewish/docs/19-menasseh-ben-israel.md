Menasseh ben Israel (1604 to 1657) was one of the rabbis of the Portuguese Jews of Amsterdam, a prolific author and printer, and the man who went to Oliver Cromwell to plead for the return of the Jews to England. Christian scholars across Europe wrote to him and came to see him. His Amsterdam was a few streets on and around Vlooienburg.

![Menasseh ben Israel, engraved by Salom Italia in Amsterdam, 1642](commons:File:Menasseh ben Israel 1642.jpg)

## From Lisbon to Amsterdam

He was born in 1604 as Manoel Dias Soeiro, probably in Lisbon, into a family of *conversos*, Jews forced to live as Catholics. The family left Portugal and, by way of Madeira and La Rochelle, reached Amsterdam when he was a child. There they returned openly to Judaism and he took the name Menasseh ben Israel. He studied with Isaac Uziel, rabbi of the congregation Neve Shalom, and around 1620, still a teenager, took over his teacher's place. In 1623 he married Rachel Abravanel; they had three children, Joseph, Samuel and Gracia.

## Printer and author

In 1626 he set up a Hebrew printing press, by most accounts the first in the city, and printed a prayer book of the Spanish rite as its first work. For three decades he printed Bibles, prayer books and his own writings, in Hebrew, Spanish, Portuguese and Latin, and helped make Amsterdam a centre of Jewish printing.

His own books were written for two audiences. *El Conciliador* (first part 1632) tried to reconcile passages of the Bible that seem to contradict one another, and was read by Christian scholars as much as by Jews. Later came books on the resurrection of the dead (1636), on human frailty and sin (1642) and, in Hebrew, *Nishmat Hayyim* (1651) on the immortality of the soul. In *Esperança de Israel* (The Hope of Israel, 1650) he discussed reports that descendants of the lost tribes of Israel lived in the Americas, and what that could mean for the coming of the Messiah.

When the three Portuguese congregations merged into Talmud Torah in 1639, Menasseh became third in rank of its four rabbis, behind Saul Levi Morteira and Isaac Aboab da Fonseca. He was also by far the worst paid: 150 guilders a year, against Morteira's 600. Money troubles followed him all his life. It is often said that he taught the young Spinoza; there is no evidence that he did.

## Rembrandt

Menasseh lived and ran his press in a house on the Nieuwe Houtmarkt, on the island of Vlooienburg (see [Vlooienburg](place:Vlooienburg under the Stopera, the plaque that took thirty years, and Spinoza)). Rembrandt lived from 1639 in a house on the Breestraat, now the [Rembrandthuis](place:Rembrandthuis (Rembrandt House Museum)), just across the Houtgracht. They were not next-door neighbours, as is often said, but they lived a few minutes apart and had friends in common.

How well they knew each other is debated. An etched portrait of a man by Rembrandt, dated 1636, has been called a portrait of Menasseh since a French catalogue of 1751, but nothing in the print shows who the sitter is, and many scholars now doubt it. Some think Menasseh helped Rembrandt with the Hebrew letters on the wall in *Belshazzar's Feast* (about 1636 to 1639). What is certain is that in 1655 Rembrandt etched four small scenes for Menasseh's book *Piedra gloriosa*, a mystical commentary on the stone in Nebuchadnezzar's dream; they appear in some copies only. The Rembrandthuis has copies with the etchings.

## The mission to England

Jews had been expelled from England in 1290. Menasseh saw a chance to change that after the English civil war, and dedicated the English edition of *The Hope of Israel* to Parliament. In the autumn of 1655 he crossed to London and presented Cromwell with an address asking for Jews to be readmitted with the right to worship and trade. In December 1655 Cromwell put the question to a conference of lawyers, clergy and merchants at Whitehall. It ended without a formal decision, but in practice the small group of Jews already in London was allowed to stay and to worship in private, which is why Menasseh is counted among the founders of the modern Jewish community in England. In 1656 he answered the old charges against the Jews in *Vindiciae Judaeorum*.

The journey cost him dearly. His son Samuel died in England in 1657, and Menasseh took the body back across the sea. He died at his brother-in-law's house in Middelburg on 20 November 1657 and was buried at [Beth Haim](place:Beth Haim, the Portuguese Jews' cemetery at Ouderkerk) in Ouderkerk.

Today the research institute of the Jewish Cultural Quarter and the two Amsterdam universities carries his name: the Menasseh ben Israel Instituut. For the community he served, see [The Portuguese Nation](doc:The Portuguese Nation).
