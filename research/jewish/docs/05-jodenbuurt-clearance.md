Amsterdammers called it the Jodenbuurt or the Jodenhoek: the Jewish quarter in the east of the old city, around the Jodenbreestraat and on the islands of Vlooienburg, Marken and Uilenburg, laid out in the 1590s. It was never a ghetto. There were no walls or gates, and Christians lived there too; Rembrandt bought his house in the Jodenbreestraat in 1639. But for three centuries most of Amsterdam's Jews lived within a few hundred metres of the synagogues on what is now the Jonas Daniël Meijerplein. Very little of it is left. This document explains why.

## Around 1900

By the end of the nineteenth century the quarter was one of the most crowded and poorest parts of the city, and also one of its busiest. Its high streets were the Jodenbreestraat and the Weesperstraat, which Yiddish speakers called the *Wazepergas*. When two canals were filled in 1882, the city moved the street traders of the Jodenbreestraat onto the new [Waterlooplein](place:Waterlooplein market, from Jewish market to flea market); from 1893 it was a daily market, closed on Saturdays. In 1934 the Waterlooplein and the Nieuwmarkt were the city's largest markets, with about 300 stalls each, and Jewish traders also worked the Uilenburgerstraat and the Amstelveld. In May 1938 nearly a third of the city's pedlars were Jewish, not counting the rag-and-bone men.

![The Jodenbreestraat around 1900, at the corner of the Valkenburgerstraat, with the back of the Mozes en Aäronkerk on the left and the Zuiderkerk in the distance](commons:File:Jodenbreestraat met op de voorgrond de kruising met de Valkenburgerstraat, Afb PBKD00205000017.jpg)

Behind the shopping streets lay alleys and courts where whole families lived in one room. In the Valkenburgerstraat, on Marken, more than three hundred people lived in one alley at no. 195, in thirty-nine one-room homes without running water or drains. The [Zonnehofje](place:Zonnehofje, Valkenburgerstraat (gone)) was one of sixteen such alleys off that street. The community's own institutions filled the gaps: a [nursery school](place:The bridge yard and the last of the Jewish nursery school) on Uilenburg for 600 children of the poorest families, schools, orphanages, a [public bathhouse](place:Splendor, the bathhouse of the Jewish island) that opened Sunday to Friday and closed on the Sabbath, and a theatre of its own, [Tip Top](place:Tip Top, the Jewish quarter's theatre (gone)), in the Jodenbreestraat. Families who could afford it were already leaving for the Plantage, Oost and later Zuid (see [Emancipation and assimilation, 1796 to 1940](doc:Emancipation and assimilation, 1796 to 1940)).

## The first clearances

The Housing Act of 1901 gave the city the power to declare homes unfit and expropriate them. The Jewish islands were among the first targets. The city began expropriating the Zonnehofje in 1901. In 1910, 368 of Uilenburg's 861 homes were declared unfit to live in, and the island became Amsterdam's first slum clearance area. Its two narrow main streets were knocked into one wide one, lined with new housing and schools. Some residents could come back: the Jewish workers' association Handwerkers Vriendenkring, founded in 1869, built [Nieuw Uilenburg](place:Nieuw Uilenburg, Handwerkers Vriendenkring blocks) in 1917 to 1922 and took over a [new block of 1927](place:Uilenburg rebuilt, the workers' block and the Witte Olifant school) for families from the old slums. In 1926 the city called the Valkenburgerstraat a disgrace and proposed to declare 532 of its homes unfit. Many families moved out to new working-class districts, among them the Transvaalbuurt in Oost.

## Emptied

In September 1941 the occupier banned Jews from the public markets; the Waterlooplein became one of four markets where only Jews could trade. Between 1941 and 1945 about 60,000 Jews were deported from Amsterdam, most of them to be murdered, and in 1943 the old quarter was sealed off and emptied in raids. Very few of its market traders survived. The empty houses were boarded up, and in the Hunger Winter of 1944 to 1945, when the city had no fuel, they were stripped for firewood. The galleries of the Great Synagogue went the same way, and so did the wood of the Tip Top theatre. In 1945 whole streets of the Jodenbuurt were ruins. The persecution itself is told in [Persecution, 1940 to 1943, and the Jewish Council](doc:Persecution, 1940 to 1943, and the Jewish Council).

## Cleared rather than rebuilt

After the war the few who came back found a quarter that, for the city, was a problem to be solved rather than a place to restore. The houses that had been stripped were demolished. The [Weesperstraat](place:Weesperstraat, the Jewish quarter's high street turned city motorway) was made into a four-lane road from the IJ tunnel to the Wibautstraat, and its remaining houses were pulled down in the late 1950s and early 1960s. The Valkenburgerstraat was given up from the 1950s for an approach road to the IJ tunnel; the [last old building on it](place:Valkenburgerstraat 202–210, the street's last old building), a block of 1877, still stands. The Joden Houttuinen went in the late 1960s, leaving only a lock-keeper's house, the [Goslerhuisje](place:Goslerhuisje, the last house of the Joden Houttuinen). In 1958 the city filled two gaps in the Rapenburgerstraat with [studio houses for artists](place:Artists' studio houses in the gaps of the Jewish quarter). The Uilenburgerstraat market never came back.

In the late 1960s the city planned a wide road and the first metro line through the Nieuwmarkt area, and bought [Huis De Pinto](place:Huis De Pinto, the house that stopped a motorway), the seventeenth-century house of a Portuguese Jewish banking family, in 1968 in order to pull it down. Residents and campaigners squatted it, and on 5 January 1972 the council dropped the road by one vote. The metro went ahead: in 1975 clearances for it led to riots in the Nieuwmarkt. The fight changed Amsterdam's planning for good, and the rest of the area was rebuilt along its old street lines.

On Vlooienburg, the old heart of the quarter, the city built its new town hall and opera house, the Stopera, finished in the late 1980s; the market moved to a smaller square beside it and became the flea market it is today. [A plaque for the island's last residents](place:Vlooienburg under the Stopera, the plaque that took thirty years, and Spinoza) came only in 2022.

## What is left

Walk from the Waterlooplein to the Nieuwmarkt and the old quarter shows itself in fragments: the four synagogues of the [Jewish Museum](place:Jewish Museum (Joods Museum), four synagogues in one) and the [Portuguese Synagogue](place:Portuguese Synagogue (Esnoga)); the [Uilenburgersjoel](place:Uilenburgersjoel, the synagogue behind the wall) behind its wall; the Hebrew texts on the [girls' orphanage](place:Jewish girls' orphanage of the Rapenburgerstraat) of the Rapenburgerstraat; the [Bussenschuthofje](place:Bussenschuthofje (former)) gate; the [rooster](place:The rooster of De Haan's matzo factory (Valkenburgerstraat)) of a matzo bakery; the Splendor bathhouse; [Huis De Pinto](place:Huis De Pinto, the house that stopped a motorway). The rest is wide roads, post-war blocks and names. The walk through Vlooienburg, Marken and Uilenburg links them in order.
