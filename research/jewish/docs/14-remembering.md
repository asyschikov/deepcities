Amsterdam has a great many memorials to the war, and the ones for its Jews tell a story of their own: who was remembered first, who later, and who decided. The early monuments spoke of resistance and gratitude. The later ones speak of names: of particular people, at particular doors. This essay walks through them in the order they came. For what they remember, see [Persecution, 1940 to 1943, and the Jewish Council](doc:Persecution, 1940 to 1943, and the Jewish Council).

## The first years: resistance and thanks

In the first postwar years the war was remembered mainly as a national story of occupation and resistance. The first large Amsterdam monument connected with the Jews was about others: the [Monument van Joodse Erkentelijkheid](place:Monument van Joodse Erkentelijkheid, the thank-you nobody wanted next door) of 1950, a thank-you from Jewish survivors to the Dutch who had helped, disputed from the start. Then, in 1952, came [De Dokwerker](place:De Dokwerker and the February Strike) on the Jonas Daniël Meijerplein, Mari Andriessen's docker, which honours the city's strike against the persecution rather than its victims (see [February 1941](doc:February 1941)). In 1959 Hildo Krop's relief for the murdered Jewish traders was unveiled in the [Centrale Markthal](place:Centrale Markthal).

## The Schouwburg

The theatre where Jews had been assembled for deportation became a memorial only in 1962. A committee had bought it in 1947 and given it to the city on condition that it would never again be used for entertainment. Its auditorium was cleared to make a courtyard with a column where the stage had stood. In 1993 a wall with about 6,700 family names was added. The [Hollandsche Schouwburg](place:Hollandsche Schouwburg, from theatre to deportation centre) is free to enter every day (see [The Hollandsche Schouwburg, the crèche and the children](doc:The Hollandsche Schouwburg, the crèche and the children)).

## Neighbourhoods remember

From the 1980s the initiative moved to neighbourhoods and survivors. In 1986 a former resistance man, alarmed by antisemitic incidents, got the [Kindermonument](place:Kindermonument, the playground that became a market for Jews) placed at the playground on the Gaaspstraat that had been turned into a market for Jews; the sculptor, Truus Menger-Oversteegen, had fought in the armed resistance. In 1987 a golden Star of David went up on the Transvaalplein, the first of several [Transvaalbuurt memorials](place:Jewish memorials of the Transvaalbuurt). In 1988 the black column of the [Monument Joods Verzet](place:Megadlé Jethomim and the Monument Joods Verzet) was placed on the Amstel, at the wish of a Jewish resistance man, and in 1989 a line of words in the pavement traced the walls of the Jewish boys' orphanage under the new town hall.

In 1993 Jan Wolkers's [broken mirrors](place:"Nooit meer Auschwitz", the broken-mirror monument (Wertheimpark)) moved to the Wertheimpark. Under six cracked glass panels lies an urn with ashes from Auschwitz; in the mirrors "the sky stays damaged for ever". The National Holocaust Remembrance ends here every last Sunday of January.

![Jan Wolkers's Auschwitz monument in the Wertheimpark](commons:File:Auschwitz monument amsterdam.JPG)

Memorials at places of departure followed: at the [Muiderpoort station](place:Muiderpoort deportation memorial) in 2002, and in 2007 a line of 65 tiles in front of the school on the site of the Zentralstelle, the SS office that organised the deportations (see [St Ignatius Gymnasium](place:St Ignatius Gymnasium, on the site of the Zentralstelle)).

## Names at the door

From 2010 the [Stolpersteine](doc:Stolpersteine) brought names into the pavement, one brass stone per person, in front of the last home they chose themselves. Residents of single streets did their own research and made their own memorials. On the Nieuwe Keizersgracht the [Schaduwkade](place:Schaduwkade, the quay that remembers) of 2013 sets about two hundred small plates into the quay edge, each opposite the house where the person lived. On the Gerard Terborgstraat, tenants of a housing cooperative found 169 names of people from their blocks and gave them the form of [empty doorposts](place:The doorposts of the Gerard Terborgstraat) (2017). At Weesperzijde 141, rather than 39 stones, the residents of a former Jewish boarding house had a bronze made of one man, [Leendert Pop](place:Leendert Pop and Pension Verdooner), as the face of all of them. The football clubs of the Watergraafsmeer have added name panels to their [memorial](place:Football clubs' war memorials, Middenmeer). Every year around 4 May, the Open Joodse Huizen programme opens homes where people lived who were taken away, and tells their stories inside.

![The Schaduwkade: plates in the quay edge, each facing a house on the Nieuwe Keizersgracht](commons:File:2022 De Schaduwkade, Amsterdam (6).jpg)

## The names monument

On 19 September 2021 King Willem-Alexander unveiled the [National Holocaust Names Monument](place:National Holocaust Names Monument (Holocaust Namenmonument)) on the Weesperstraat, on land left empty when the old Jewish quarter was cleared. Designed by Daniel Libeskind, its brick walls carry about 102,000 names of Jews from the Netherlands who were murdered and have no grave, and some 220 Sinti and Roma, each with a date of birth and an age at death. Seen from above, the walls form the Hebrew letters of the word for "in memory of". The idea came from the Dutch Auschwitz Committee in 2006; it took fifteen years.

![The National Holocaust Names Monument on the Weesperstraat](commons:File:Holocaust Namenmonument (Amsterdam).jpg)

## Recent years

The pace has not slowed. A [mirror at the Vondelpark gate](place:The mirror at the Vondelpark gate (Van Eeghenstraat)) (2021) marks where a "Forbidden for Jews" sign hung. Ram Katzir's [shadows on the Olympiaplein](place:Schaduwen, the shadows of the Olympiaplein raid) (2022) are cut from a photograph of the raid of 20 June 1943. A plate in the pavement by the town hall remembers the 393 children of [Vlooienburg](place:Vlooienburg under the Stopera, the plaque that took thirty years, and Spinoza), after a wait of almost thirty years. The [National Holocaust Museum](place:National Holocaust Museum (Nationaal Holocaustmuseum), in the old teacher-training college) opened in 2024. In 2025 stumbling thresholds were laid at three tram stops from which Jews were taken to the trains, and the mayor apologised for the city's role in the persecution. In September 2026 the university unveiled a [ring of names](place:University of Amsterdam names memorial (Oudemanhuispoort)) of its murdered students and staff.

## Questions that remain

Each memorial has raised questions: who is named and who is left out, whether the dead should be under one's feet, whether a thank-you to helpers fits a city where most Jews were not saved, and who should be asked when a memorial comes to a street. The Monument van Joodse Erkentelijkheid has moved three times; when it was proposed for a small park in 2017, residents objected, and it went back to the Weesperplein in 2021 with a plaque explaining its disputed past. These debates are part of how the city remembers.
