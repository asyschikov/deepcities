Before the war Amsterdam had dozens of synagogues and prayer rooms, from monumental buildings seating more than a thousand to a single room upstairs in a house. Their story follows the Jews of the city: first on the islands of the old Jodenbuurt, then out to the Plantage, Oost and the Pijp, then to the new districts of Zuid. Most were emptied in 1943; many were never used again. This guide goes through them roughly in the order they were built. Synagogue, *sjoel* in Amsterdam speech and *esnoga* among the Portuguese, all mean the same: a house of prayer and study, not a temple.

## The great synagogues on the square

The Portuguese worshipped first in houses, in three congregations that merged in 1639 as Talmud Torah; their synagogue was on the Houtgracht until 1675. That year they consecrated the [Esnoga](place:Portuguese Synagogue (Esnoga)), the Portuguese Synagogue designed by Elias Bouman, then among the largest synagogues in the world, with room for 1,200 men and 440 women. It is still lit only by candles and daylight, has a floor of sand, and still holds services; its story is told in [The Portuguese Nation](doc:The Portuguese Nation).

Across the square the Ashkenazim had already built theirs. The Great Synagogue of 1671, by Bouman and Daniel Stalpaert, was followed by the Obbene Sjoel (1685), the Dritt Sjoel (1700, rebuilt 1778) and the Neie Sjoel (1730, rebuilt 1752). Together they formed one block of synagogues on the square. The last service in the Great Synagogue was held on 28 September 1943. In the Hunger Winter its wooden galleries were torn out for fuel. After the war the shrunken community could not use the buildings; the city bought them, and since 1987 they have housed the [Jewish Museum](place:Jewish Museum (Joods Museum), four synagogues in one), which began in 1932 in the Waag.

## Hidden in the old quarter

Away from the square, smaller synagogues served particular neighbourhoods and groups. On Uilenburg a house synagogue opened in 1724, and in 1766 the community built the [Uilenburgersjoel](place:Uilenburgersjoel, the synagogue behind the wall), set back from the street behind a wall and reached through an alley. It was used until 1943. The city bought it in 1954, and since 1997 the Jewish congregation Beit Ha'Chidush has used it. A few streets away the [Rapenburgersjoel](place:Rapenburgersjoel, the synagogue of the 1797 breakaway) served the breakaway congregation of 1797, and the [Beth Hamidrasj Ets Haim](place:Beth Hamidrasj Ets Haim, house of learning with a synagogue at the back) in the Rapenburgerstraat, a house of learning since 1883, had a synagogue at the back.

In the Nieuwe Kerkstraat, behind the Weesperstraat, the congregation Neir Mitswoh Wetouro Our moved in 1913 into [Harry Elte's first synagogue](place:Harry Elte's first synagogue (Nieuwe Kerkstraat 10–14)), with 192 seats for men and 26 for women behind an arched window that is still the only clue. Further along, Jews from the Russian Empire, who came after the pogroms of the 1880s, took a house with a bell gable as their own synagogue in 1889, the [Russensjoel](place:The Russensjoel and the old "Jodenkerkstraat" (Nieuwe Kerkstraat 149)). In the Weesperzijde, another Eastern European congregation founded in 1890 prayed in the [Blasiussjoel](place:Blasiussjoel, Swammerdamstraat 70), which reopened after the war and kept the Eastern European rite in memory of its murdered members.

One synagogue went into hiding. After the occupier dissolved the Portuguese congregation in February 1944, its sexton, allowed back from Westerbork, kept a [hidden synagogue](place:Hidden synagogue of the Nieuwe Keizersgracht (nos. 33 and 37)) in his flat at Nieuwe Keizersgracht 33 from the middle of that year until liberation. Up to thirty men came on a Sabbath.

## Oost and the Pijp

As families left the old quarter in the late nineteenth century, synagogues followed them. In the Pijp, then a district of diamond workers, the architect E.M. Rood built the [Gerard Dou Synagogue](place:Gerard Dou Synagogue) in 1891 to 1892 for the congregation Tesjoengas Israel, squeezed into a row of houses; it was known as the diamond shul, survived the war and is still used. In the Dapperbuurt the architect Jacob S. Baars turned the lower floors of a house in the Commelinstraat into a [small synagogue](place:Former synagogue, Commelinstraat 16) in 1921 to 1922, meant to serve only until Oost had a big one. That came in September 1928: [Synagoge Oost](place:Synagoge Oost, Linnaeusstraat (gone)) on the Linnaeusstraat, also by Baars, set at 45 degrees to the street so that the Ark faced Jerusalem. Wrecked in the war and left empty, it was demolished in 1962, and its stained glass went to Israel.

## Zuid

Between the wars Zuid became the new centre of Jewish Amsterdam, and two synagogues in the Amsterdam School style were built there. The first, in the Jacob Obrechtstraat, was designed by Harry Elte and consecrated on 18 May 1928. Elte himself was murdered in Theresienstadt in 1944. Restored in 1997 and now named after Aron Schuster, chief rabbi from 1955 to 1973, the [Obrechtsjoel](place:Obrechtsjoel (Raw Aron Schuster Synagogue)) is the largest Ashkenazi synagogue in use in the Netherlands.

![The entrance of the Raw Aron Schuster Synagogue in the Jacob Obrechtstraat](commons:File:Ingangspartij van de Raw Aron Schuster Sjoel te Amsterdam - Amsterdam - 20409330 - RCE.jpg)

The second, in the Rivierenbuurt, where the Jewish population rose from about 4,000 in 1930 to 17,000 in 1940, was the [Lekstraat synagogue](place:Lekstraat synagogue) by Abraham Elzas, inaugurated on 30 November 1937. From 1943 the occupier used it to store furniture taken from deported Jews. From 1985 to 1999 part of the building housed the Verzetsmuseum, the resistance museum; it was restored in 2006, and a small synagogue in the building is in daily use.

## Liberal Amsterdam

Liberal Judaism came to Amsterdam in the 1930s, largely with refugees from Germany. From 1937 the young Liberal congregation rented the former Theosophical temple on the Tolstraat, now the [Cinétol](place:Theosophical temple on the Tolstraat (Cinétol)), a concrete building by Brinkman and Van der Vlugt. After the war it began again almost from nothing. In 1957 it opened a synagogue on the upper floor of a house in the [De Lairessestraat](place:Liberal synagogue, De Lairessestraat 145), and under rabbi Jacob Soetendorp grew so fast that in 1966 it built its own on the Graafschapstraat. In 2010 it moved into a new building by SeARCH on the street now named after Soetendorp, with [room for 800 people](place:The Liberal Jewish synagogue on Jacob Soetendorp's island) and a menorah cut into its glass front.

## Beyond the city

[Weesp](place:Former synagogue, Nieuwstraat), now part of Amsterdam, had Jews from at least the 1770s and built a synagogue in the Nieuwstraat in 1840 that also served Muiden and the surrounding villages. After the war it was a garage for many years; it has been a synagogue again since 1997.

## Where to go now

Of the buildings in this guide, the Esnoga, the Gerard Dou Synagogue, the Obrechtsjoel, the Lekstraat synagogue, the Uilenburgersjoel, the Weesp synagogue and the Liberal synagogue still hold services. The Esnoga and the Jewish Museum are open to visitors; the others can be seen from the street, and some open for services or on heritage days. The Jewish community of today is described in [Jewish Amsterdam today](doc:Jewish Amsterdam today).
