Amsterdam called them the *Hoogduitsers*, the High Germans, to tell them apart from the Portuguese. They were Ashkenazi Jews: Jews whose families came from the German lands and, later, from Poland, Lithuania and further east. They arrived later than the Portuguese, poorer, and in far larger numbers, and by the end of the eighteenth century they made up five in six of the city's Jews. Most of the Jewish Amsterdam that is remembered today, the Jodenbuurt of market stalls and alleys, of Yiddish words and diamond workers, is theirs.

## Arrival

The first Ashkenazim settled in Amsterdam in the early decades of the seventeenth century, drawn by the booming city and pushed out of the German lands by war, persecution and hard times. At first they depended on the Portuguese, who employed them as servants and workers and helped their poor. In 1635 they founded their own congregation, and in 1642 they bought land for a cemetery at Muiderberg, on the Zuiderzee coast east of the city.

A second wave came from further east, after the massacres of 1648 and 1649 in Ukraine and the wars between Russia, Poland and Sweden in the 1650s. These Polish Jews formed a congregation of their own in 1660, much to the displeasure of the German one, and bought a burial plot next to it at Muiderberg. In 1673 the city ordered the two to merge. The single High German congregation that resulted is, as the Nederlands-Israëlietische Hoofdsynagoge, still the largest Jewish congregation in the Netherlands.

The Portuguese did not hide their sense of superiority. In 1642 they set up a workhouse, Abodat Hahesed, to keep German and Polish Jews off the streets, where begging could land them in the city's house of correction; it ran until 1670, when the Portuguese decided the Ashkenazim could look after their own poor. Relations stayed cool for a long time. Even well into the twentieth century the Portuguese looked down on the "Germans".

## Four synagogues on one square

In 1671, four years before the Portuguese, the Ashkenazim consecrated their Great Synagogue, designed by Elias Bouman with the city architect Daniel Stalpaert, with room for 399 men and 368 women. It stood across the water from the site where the Esnoga would rise. After the Polish congregation joined, and as immigration went on, more space was needed: the Obbene Sjoel ("upstairs synagogue") followed in 1685, the Dritt Sjoel ("third") in 1700, rebuilt in 1778, and the Neie Sjoel ("new") in 1730, rebuilt in 1752 and in the end bigger than the Great one. Together they formed a block of synagogues on what is now the Jonas Daniël Meijerplein. Since 1987 they have housed the [Jewish Museum](place:Jewish Museum (Joods Museum), four synagogues in one).

![The Great Synagogue of the High German Jews, in a print of 1693 to 1694](commons:File:Gezicht op de Grote Synagoge te Amsterdam De Hoogduytse Ioode Synagoge (titel op object), RP-P-AO-24-40A.jpg)

The poorest lived further north, on the islands laid out in the 1590s. On Uilenburg a house synagogue opened in 1724, and in 1766 the community built the [Uilenburgersjoel](place:Uilenburgersjoel, the synagogue behind the wall), reached through an alley, with the prayer hall upstairs and rooms for wedding parties below. It still stands, behind a wall on the Nieuwe Uilenburgerstraat. In 1772 the High German community owned the five little houses of the [Kokshofje](place:Kokshofje (Klokkenhofje), Uilenburg (former)) beside it. Around these large synagogues, many smaller prayer rooms, *chevrot*, met in houses throughout the quarter; the [Synagogues of Amsterdam](doc:Synagogues of Amsterdam) guide follows them out of the old town.

## The poor majority

The Ashkenazim grew fast. Amsterdam had about 1,000 of them in 1650 and 9,000 in 1725. By 1750 there were 14,000 Ashkenazim against 4,583 Portuguese Jews, and a census at the end of the century counted about 24,000 Jews in the city, 20,000 of them Ashkenazim. The census takers of 1795 complained that they could not count the Jewish quarter properly: every room, up to the attic, was so crowded that they could not vouch that no one, children above all, had been missed.

Most were very poor. In the late eighteenth century 87 per cent of the Ashkenazi community received poor relief. Excluded from the guilds, they could not work in most crafts. They became street traders and stallholders, pedlars of old clothes, combs and spectacles, servants, porters and workers in industries the guilds did not cover. Street trading and markets were their speciality, in a way they never were for the Portuguese. Fear of Jewish competition led Christian diamond cutters in 1748 to ask the city for a guild that would shut Jews out of the trade; the city refused, and diamonds were to become a Jewish trade (see [Diamonds and the ANDB](doc:Diamonds and the ANDB)). A small group of families, among them the Gomperts, Goldsmid and Van Oven, grew rich from international trade with the German lands, Eastern Europe and London.

Prejudice was real but rarely violent. The word *smous* (probably from *Mousje*, little Moses) became a common term of abuse for a Jewish cheat, used in plays, prints and verse; during the stock crash of 1720 Jewish brokers were attacked. None of it led to the expulsions or ghettos known elsewhere in Europe.

## Learning, printing, Yiddish

Ashkenazi education began at home, with teachers supervised by the chief rabbi. In 1660 the congregation opened a school, Talmud Torah like the Portuguese one, mainly for poor children. In 1740 a new chief rabbi from Poland, Arjeh Leib Loewenstamm, founded a house of learning, the Beth Hamidrash Ets Haim, at first in the Dritt Sjoel; in 1883 it moved to the [Rapenburgerstraat](place:Beth Hamidrasj Ets Haim, house of learning with a synagogue at the back). Until well into the nineteenth century the community's rabbis were brought in from Germany, Bohemia and Poland.

Amsterdam was the world centre of Hebrew and Yiddish printing in this period, and the language of the Ashkenazi quarter was Yiddish, in its western Dutch variant. In the nineteenth century the government and the Jewish leadership set out to replace it with Dutch, and within a century it was gone (see [Emancipation and assimilation, 1796 to 1940](doc:Emancipation and assimilation, 1796 to 1940)). What survived were words taken into Amsterdam speech: *mazzel* (luck), *stiekem* (on the sly), *gein* (fun), *lef* (nerve), *smoes* (excuse), *gotspe* (cheek), and *Mokum* itself, from Hebrew *makom*, "place", the Jews' name for their city. More of them in [Mokum: Jewish words and culture in Amsterdam](doc:Mokum: Jewish words and culture in Amsterdam).

## The cemeteries

Burial was a measure of status. Muiderberg was far and the journey by barge expensive, so in 1714 the community opened a second cemetery at [Zeeburg](place:Jewish cemetery Zeeburg), just east of the city, for the poor who could not pay synagogue dues, for migrants, for children under thirteen and for those who died too close to the Sabbath or a holiday to make the trip. With Ashkenazi immigration Zeeburg became the largest Jewish cemetery in the Netherlands: about 95,000 people were buried there between 1714 and 1942. When it was full, in 1914, a new cemetery opened in [Diemen](place:Jewish cemetery Diemen, where the Zeeburg graves went). After the war part of Zeeburg was cleared and the rest neglected; only around a hundred stones remain. [Muiderberg](place:Jewish cemetery Muiderberg, the largest still in use), with some 45,000 graves, is still in use.

## Into the nineteenth century

In 1795, with the Batavian Revolution, a small group of mainly Ashkenazi progressives founded the society Felix Libertate to fight for Jewish civil rights. After emancipation in 1796 they demanded new statutes for the congregation; when the leaders refused, they broke away and from 1799 prayed in the [Rapenburgersjoel](place:Rapenburgersjoel, the synagogue of the 1797 breakaway). That story, and how the poor Ashkenazi majority became Dutch Jews, continues in [Emancipation and assimilation, 1796 to 1940](doc:Emancipation and assimilation, 1796 to 1940) and [The Jodenbuurt and its clearance](doc:The Jodenbuurt and its clearance).
