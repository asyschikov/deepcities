Amsterdammers call their city Mokum, and many who say it do not know that the word is Yiddish. It is one sign among many of how deeply Jewish Amsterdam, above all the poor Ashkenazi Jews of the old quarter east of the Amstel, shaped the city's speech, humour, trade and entertainment. This essay looks at that everyday culture before 1940 and at what is left of it. For the longer history behind it, see [Jewish Amsterdam, four centuries](doc:Jewish Amsterdam, four centuries).

## Mokum and other words

Mokum comes from the Yiddish *mokem*, from the Hebrew *makom*, place. In Bargoens, the old Dutch thieves' cant that borrowed heavily from Yiddish, it simply meant a town: it is recorded in that sense in a word list of 1731. Towns were told apart by the Hebrew letter of their initial, so Amsterdam was Mokum Alef, the A-town. *Mokumer* for an Amsterdammer is first recorded in 1906, and only in the 20th century did Mokum on its own come to mean Amsterdam; a popular song of 1955, Johnny Jordaan's "Geef mij maar Amsterdam", probably helped.

Many more Yiddish words entered Amsterdam speech through Jewish neighbours, street trade and Bargoens, and from Amsterdam they spread across the Netherlands. *Mazzel* (luck), *sores* (worries), *mesjogge* (mad), *geintje* (a joke), *tof* (great), *lef* (nerve) and *smoes* (an excuse) are now ordinary Dutch. *Jatten*, to steal, comes from *jad*, hand. The underworld words *penoze* and *smeris* (a policeman) took the same road. Hans Beem collected such words in *Resten van een taal* (Remnants of a language, 1959). Before the war about one in ten Amsterdammers was Jewish; with the murder of at least three quarters of them, much of this speech left the streets, but the words stayed in the language.

## Markets and matzo

Much of Jewish Amsterdam made its living in the street. Jewish street traders sold from carts and stalls in and around the Jodenbreestraat. From 1893 their market had a fixed place on the new [Waterlooplein](place:Waterlooplein market, from Jewish market to flea market), open six days a week and closed on Saturday, the Sabbath. After the war it came back as the flea market that is still there.

![A couple behind their handcart on the Waterlooplein market, 1909](commons:File:Steden, SFA022009601.jpg)

Food was part of the trade and of the calendar. The De Haan bakery in the Valkenburgerstraat made five to six million matzos a year before the war, the unleavened bread of Passover; its [rooster sign](place:The rooster of De Haan's matzo factory (Valkenburgerstraat)) survives inside the new building on the site. In Weesp, Bakker Gans in the [Slijkstraat](place:Slijkstraat) was a kosher bakery with a hall where Amsterdam Jewish couples came to marry and celebrate.

![A street hawker selling stockings in the Nieuwe Uilenburgerstraat, around 1930](commons:File:Stadsarchief Amsterdam, Afb ANWI00525000001.jpg)

## Stage and screen

The Jewish quarter had its own entertainment. The [Tip Top](place:Tip Top, the Jewish quarter's theatre (gone)) in the Jodenbreestraat, opened in 1914, showed films and put on cabaret and variety; the entertainers Louis and Heintje Davids played there. The Plantage Middenlaan was a theatre street: the [Hollandsche Schouwburg](place:Hollandsche Schouwburg, from theatre to deportation centre), and a few doors along the house now called [Desmet](place:Desmet, the theatre of many names (Plantage Middenlaan 4)). Earlier, in 1876, the Jewish men's choir Oefening Baart Kunst had built its own concert hall nearby, [Plancius](place:Plancius, the choir's clubhouse (Verzetsmuseum)), which now houses the Resistance Museum.

The grandest cinema in the city was built by a Jewish immigrant. Abraham Tuschinski, born in Poland in 1886, came to the Netherlands on his way to America and stayed. With his brothers-in-law he opened the [Tuschinski](place:Pathé Tuschinski) theatre on the Reguliersbreestraat in 1921. In 1940 the occupier took the company from them, and in 1942 all three were murdered.

![The front of the Tuschinski theatre](commons:File:Tuschinski front.jpg)

Two young employees of the Jewish-owned department store [De Bijenkorf](place:De Bijenkorf on the Dam), Nol van Wesel and Max Kannewasser, were discovered at a staff party in 1934 and became the jazz duo Johnny & Jones. They were deported in 1944 and died in the camps weeks before the end of the war. When the occupation came, Jewish performers were first restricted to Jewish audiences, in the renamed Joodsche Schouwburg and the Theater van de Lach in the Desmet building, and then deported.

## Music and art

In April 1941 the occupier ordered the Concertgebouw to dismiss the seventeen Jewish members of its orchestra; three were murdered. Their names are on a [memorial](place:Memorial to the Jewish musicians, Concertgebouw) behind the Main Hall. The artists' society [Arti et Amicitiae](place:Arti et Amicitiae, the artists' club on the corner of the Spui) expelled fifteen Jewish members on the occupier's orders; only three of them survived the war, and in 2022 its chairman apologised. The graphic artist Fré Cohen, who designed much of the city's printed matter in the 1930s, left a small mark you can still find: the [plaques](place:Fré Cohen's level-crossing plaques) on the railway underpasses in Oost.

## Sport

The Olympic Games of 1928 were held in Amsterdam, and the first women's gymnastics title went to the Dutch team in the [Olympic Stadium](place:Olympic Stadium). Five of its members were Jewish, as was their coach, Gerrit Kleerekoper. Four of the five and Kleerekoper were murdered in 1943. Clubs with many Jewish members, such as HEDW and Wilhelmina Vooruit, lost a large share of their members; their [memorial](place:Football clubs' war memorials, Middenmeer) in the Watergraafsmeer carries the names. The Jewish winger Eddy Hamel, who played 125 matches for Ajax at the old ground near [De Meer](place:Stadion De Meer (gone)), was murdered in Auschwitz in 1943. The popular swimming pool in Zuid carries the name of the Jewish alderman who opened it in 1932, Monne de Miranda (see [De Mirandabad](place:De Mirandabad, the Mediterranean of 1979)).

## What remains

The culture described here belonged mostly to people who were murdered, and to a quarter that was cleared after the war (see [The Jodenbuurt and its clearance](doc:The Jodenbuurt and its clearance)). What remains are the words, a flea market on the old square, a few signs and buildings, and the names on the memorials. The [Jewish Museum](place:Jewish Museum (Joods Museum), four synagogues in one), which began in the [Waag](place:Waag on the Nieuwmarkt, city gate, guild house and first Jewish museum) in 1932, keeps much of the rest. For the labour movement and the diamond trade, see [Henri Polak](doc:Henri Polak).
