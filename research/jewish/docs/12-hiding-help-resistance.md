The best-known Amsterdam story of the war is a story of hiding: eight people in the back annexe of Prinsengracht 263. It is also easy to draw the wrong lesson from it. Hiding was the main way Dutch Jews survived, but most never had the chance, and of those who did, many were caught. This essay gives the numbers first, then the places.

## The numbers

Estimates of how many Jews went into hiding in the Netherlands run from about 25,000 to 30,000, among them at least 4,500 children. About a third of them were found, arrested and deported. According to the figures the NIOD uses, about 16,000 Jews survived the war in hiding. Set against the roughly 140,000 Jews in the country in 1941, that is about one in nine. Most never had the chance. For the steps that led to the deportations, see [Persecution, 1940 to 1943, and the Jewish Council](doc:Persecution, 1940 to 1943, and the Jewish Council).

## How it worked

Hiding needed a room, food and papers, and each of these needed other people. Food came through ration cards, which had to be stolen or forged, because a person in hiding had none. A false identity card had to survive a check against the city's population register. On the night of 27 March 1943 a group of artists and students led by the sculptor Gerrit van der Veen and the writer Willem Arondéus set fire to that register in a building next to Artis, to make such checks harder. About fifteen per cent of the cards were destroyed. Most of the group were betrayed within weeks, and twelve were shot on 1 July 1943. A [plaque](place:The population register attack and the artists' resistance monument) on the building names them.

![The plaque for the attack on the population register, Plantage Kerklaan](commons:File:Plantage Kerklaan 36 - bevolkingsregister.jpg)

Hiding places were wherever someone dared. In the [Anne Frank House](place:Anne Frank House) eight people lived in the back annexe from July 1942 until they were arrested on 4 August 1944; all eight were deported, and only Otto Frank survived. In a large canal house on the Nieuwezijds Voorburgwal the medical student [Tina Strobos](place:Nieuwezijds Voorburgwal 282, where Tina Strobos hid more than a hundred people) and her mother sheltered more than a hundred people over the years, never more than five at a time; the house was searched eight times. In the zoo, [Artis](place:Artis), somewhere between 150 and 300 people hid in the grounds, and none was caught. The cellars of the [Centrale Markthal](place:Centrale Markthal) hid Jews until the liberation. The joking frog on [Prinsengracht 535](place:The frog stone of Prinsengracht 535) recalls a canal house where more than eight people hid. And the photograph most often shown as "Jews in hiding", taken in the cellar of [Nes 118](place:Nes 118, the cellar in the famous hiding photo) in May 1945, in fact shows the caretakers who hid thirteen people there: those they had hidden did not want to be photographed.

## Children

Children were often placed apart from their parents, with families far from the city who could pass them off as relatives or evacuees. The Amsterdamse Studenten Groep around Piet Meerburg, working with other groups, took children to hiding places outside the city; after the war its members founded the cinema [Kriterion](place:Kriterion, the cinema the resistance students built). Student groups and others took several hundred children out of the crèche opposite the Hollandsche Schouwburg (see [The Hollandsche Schouwburg, the crèche and the children](doc:The Hollandsche Schouwburg, the crèche and the children)). The teacher Joop Westerweel, chairman of a korfball club in Amsterdam, led a group that found addresses for Jewish children and ran escape routes south through Belgium and France; he was shot at Vught in August 1944, and his [plaque](place:Joop Westerweel's plaque, Blauw-Wit sports hall) hangs at his club's hall. Before the war, Truus Wijsmuller had brought hundreds of Jewish children out of Germany and Austria; her [bust](place:Truus Wijsmuller's bust, Bachplein) stands on the square where she met people who needed help during the occupation. A [monument in Buitenveldert](place:Monument for the hidden child and the protector) shows a man holding a small girl and her suitcase; its maker, Naomi Elburg, had been a hidden child herself.

![Monument for the hidden child and the protector, Buitenveldert](commons:File:Amsterdam monument ondergedoken kind en beschermer.jpg)

After the war, many hidden children had no parents to return to, and the question of where they should grow up became a bitter dispute; see [Return and the postwar years](doc:Return and the postwar years).

## Betrayal

Hiding was dangerous for both sides. People were betrayed by neighbours, by acquaintances, and by others in hiding who had been caught. Some Dutch police officers and civilians made a business of it. In 1943 the Colonne Henneicke, a unit of Dutch bounty hunters working for the occupier, was paid 7.50 guilders for every Jew it found. The resistance man Gerhard Badrian was sold for a thousand guilders by a woman he trusted and shot in front of [Rubensstraat 26](place:Gerhard Badrian's stone, Rubensstraat 26) in 1944. Helpers who were caught risked prison, camps or worse.

## Jews who resisted

Jews were not only hidden; many hid others, forged papers or fought. Badrian, a refugee from Germany, put on an SD uniform and false papers to take arrested people off police vans. The sisters Janny and Lien Brilleslijper, from the old Jewish quarter, sheltered Jews in a house in the Gooi until they were betrayed in 1944 (the family's [stumbling stones](place:The Brilleslijper house (Nieuwe Achtergracht 14)) are on the Nieuwe Achtergracht). Others hid themselves and still kept their religious life going, as in the [hidden synagogue of the Nieuwe Keizersgracht](place:Hidden synagogue of the Nieuwe Keizersgracht (nos. 33 and 37)) in 1944 and 1945. The black column of the [Monument Joods Verzet](place:Megadlé Jethomim and the Monument Joods Verzet) on the Amstel, of 1988, was the wish of the Jewish resistance boxer Bennie Bluhm and was put up to answer the old idea that Jews did not resist; the yearly Kristallnacht commemoration is held there.

## Remembering the helpers

Many helpers have been recognised by Yad Vashem as Righteous Among the Nations, among them Johan van Hulst of the teacher-training college and Joop and Willy Westerweel. Their recognition rests on files of evidence, and it is a reminder that what they did was rare. For how the city remembers both the dead and the helpers, see [Remembering: monuments, names and stones](doc:Remembering: monuments, names and stones).
