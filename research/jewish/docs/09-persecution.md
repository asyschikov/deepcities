When German troops occupied the Netherlands in May 1940, about 80,000 of the country's 140,000 Jews lived in Amsterdam, roughly one in ten of the city's people. More than 10,000 of them were refugees who had fled Germany and Austria in the 1930s. Three years later almost none were left in their homes. The persecution did not come in one blow. It came as a long series of measures, each announced in print and each carried out by offices and people, many of them Dutch. This essay follows those steps through the city. For the long view, see [Jewish Amsterdam, four centuries](doc:Jewish Amsterdam, four centuries).

## Counting and mapping

In early 1941 every person with one or more Jewish grandparents had to register with the municipality. By August 1941 the national count stood at 140,552 people classed as fully Jewish, plus some 20,000 of "mixed" descent. In Amsterdam the registration ran through the city's population register, which the historian Jeroen Kemperman, in his 2025 study for the NIOD, calls the administrative foundation of the persecution.

In May 1941 the city's statistics bureau drew a map for the occupier: the distribution of Jews over the municipality, one dot for every ten people. It shows, street by street, where the deportations would later go: the old Jewish quarter around the Waterlooplein, the Plantage, the Transvaalbuurt in Oost, and the new streets of Zuid and the Rivierenbuurt.

![The "dot map" of May 1941: each dot stands for ten Jewish residents of Amsterdam](commons:File:Verspreiding van de Joden over de gemeente Amsterdam per mei 1941.jpg)

The German civil authority in the city sat in a villa on the Museumplein, [Museumplein 19](place:Museumplein 19: from the Reich's envoy to the American consulate). Its first head, Hans Böhmcker, ordered both the registration and, in February 1941, the founding of the Jewish Council.

## Shut out, one place at a time

From 1941 the measures piled up. Signs reading "Voor Joden verboden" went up at parks, cafés, swimming pools and cinemas; one hung on the lamp post at the Vondelpark gate, where [a mirror](place:The mirror at the Vondelpark gate (Van Eeghenstraat)) now marks the spot. Jews were barred from [Artis](place:Artis) in September 1941. In April 1941 the Concertgebouw had to dismiss the Jewish members of its orchestra ([the memorial](place:Memorial to the Jewish musicians, Concertgebouw) names them), and the university had to dismiss Jewish staff ([its new memorial](place:University of Amsterdam names memorial (Oudemanhuispoort)) remembers them).

From 1 September 1941 Jewish children could only attend Jewish schools, with Jewish teachers. Some went to new schools such as the [Joods Lyceum](place:The Jewish schools of the Stadstimmertuin (Joods Lyceum)), where Anne and Margot Frank were pupils; in one primary school on the Weteringschans [a wall was built through the middle of the building](place:The wall in the middle of the school, Weteringschans 263). From November 1941 Jews could buy and sell only at separate markets, such as the playground on the Gaaspstraat, now the [Kindermonument](place:Kindermonument, the playground that became a market for Jews). From early May 1942 Jews had to wear a yellow star.

Property went too. Jews had to hand over money, shares, jewellery and art to a robbery bank that the occupier set up on the Sarphatistraat under the stolen name of a respected Jewish firm, [Lippmann, Rosenthal & Co.](place:Lippmann, Rosenthal & Co., the bank whose name was stolen). In 1942 alone the contents of nearly 10,000 Amsterdam homes were taken and shipped to Germany. Jewish men were sent to labour projects, some of them in the [Amsterdamse Bos](place:Zitplastiek voor herinnering: Jewish forced labour at the edge of the Bos), and in October 1942 the men in the labour camps and their families were sent to Westerbork and on to Auschwitz within weeks.

## The Jewish Council

After street fighting in the Jewish quarter in February 1941 (see [February 1941](doc:February 1941)), Böhmcker summoned the diamond industrialist Abraham Asscher and ordered a Judenrat. Asscher asked the classicist David Cohen to share the chair, and on 13 February 1941 twenty men met in Asscher's office. They saw themselves as a committee that would pass on orders without being responsible for them, and would refuse "dishonourable" tasks. Neither principle held.

The Joodsche Raad (Jewish Council) became the only channel between the occupier and the Jews. Its weekly paper, Het Joodsche Weekblad, printed each new decree. It ran the Jewish schools, gave out relief, distributed the yellow stars and, once deportations began, sent out call-up notices and organised the luggage, food and staff at the assembly points. It was controlled by the Zentralstelle für jüdische Auswanderung, the SS office that organised the deportations from a school on the Adama van Scheltemaplein, on the site of today's [St Ignatius Gymnasium](place:St Ignatius Gymnasium, on the site of the Zentralstelle). The Council's liaison office, the Expositur, sat nearby in the Jan van Eijckstraat. Its head office was the canal house at [Nieuwe Keizersgracht 58](place:Nieuwe Keizersgracht 58, head office of the Jewish Council).

By April 1943 the Council had about 8,000 employees in Amsterdam. Working for it brought a stamp in your identity card that exempted you from deportation for the time being. About 17,500 Council staff and some 15,000 other people held such exemptions, the "Sperre", for a while. The occupier set the numbers; the Council chose the names. The exemptions bought time and nothing more. The German police chief Willy Lages later said that without the Council they would never have managed it. The writer [Etty Hillesum](doc:Etty Hillesum) worked briefly in one of its departments.

Whether Asscher and Cohen could have done otherwise has been argued about since 1945. In 1947 a Jewish court of honour ruled that they should never again hold office in the Jewish community; a criminal investigation by the Dutch state was eventually dropped. Historians such as Jacques Presser judged the Council harshly; more recent work, such as Laurien Vastenhout's comparison of the councils in the Netherlands, Belgium and France, sets it beside the councils elsewhere in Western Europe. This map does not settle the question, but it shows where the Council worked.

## The deportations

On 5 July 1942 the first call-up notices went out, mostly to young people, for "labour in Germany". From mid-July the trains to the transit camp Westerbork ran; from there they went on to Auschwitz and, in 1943, to Sobibor. People who did not come were fetched. The [Hollandsche Schouwburg](place:Hollandsche Schouwburg, from theatre to deportation centre) became the main assembly point (see [The Hollandsche Schouwburg, the crèche and the children](doc:The Hollandsche Schouwburg, the crèche and the children)). From there and from other collecting points, the city's own trams took people to the stations, and the tram company sent the occupier the bill; [stumbling thresholds](place:The stumbling threshold at the Artis tram stop (Plantage Middenlaan)) now mark three of those stops. From October 1942 trains also left from the [Muiderpoort station](place:Muiderpoort deportation memorial), more than 11,000 people in all.

The city's offices were part of this machinery. Kemperman's study shows how the population register, the social services, the municipal police, the tram company and the health service each played a part, often on German orders given straight to the heads of service, who could act without consulting the mayor. Resistance inside the municipality existed, but it was rare, covert and mostly at the lower levels.

In 1943 the great raids emptied whole neighbourhoods. On 26 May the old quarter was sealed off; a neighbour photographed one such raid on the [Geldersekade](place:Geldersekade 105 and the raid of 26 May 1943). On 20 June about 5,500 people were taken from Zuid and Oost in one day, assembled on squares such as the [Olympiaplein](place:Schaduwen, the shadows of the Olympiaplein raid) and the Victorieplein. On 29 September 1943, the eve of the Jewish New Year, the last raid took the Council's own leaders to Westerbork, and the Council ceased to exist.

Of the Jews of Amsterdam, about three in four were murdered. Of the more than 34,000 people deported from the Netherlands to Sobibor, eighteen survived. Their names, with those of all 102,000 Dutch Jews murdered, are now on the [National Holocaust Names Monument](place:National Holocaust Names Monument (Holocaust Namenmonument)). Some escaped this machinery by going into hiding; see [Hiding, help and resistance](doc:Hiding, help and resistance).
