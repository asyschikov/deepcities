Before 1796 the Jews of Amsterdam were a tolerated nation: they ran their own affairs under their own leaders, the *parnassim*, but they were shut out of the guilds, out of public office and, for the most part, out of the city's political life. In a century and a half that changed completely. By 1900 Jewish children went to state schools, spoke Dutch, and could become lawyers, professors, members of parliament or ministers. This document follows that change, and asks how far it went.

## The decree of 1796

The French invasion of 1795 brought the Patriots to power and created the Batavian Republic. That year a small society, Felix Libertate ("happy through freedom"), was founded in Amsterdam to win civil rights for Jews. Most of its members were Ashkenazim, a few Portuguese; a third were not Jewish. It had the Declaration of the Rights of Man translated into Yiddish and lobbied the new National Assembly. On 2 September 1796 the Assembly decided that no Jew would be excluded from any right of a Batavian citizen. Jews could vote and stand for election, hold any office, settle anywhere, and work in any trade. Only France had done this before.

The old Jewish leadership was against it, because emancipation also ended the self-government of the Jewish nations and the authority of their leaders and rabbis. In Amsterdam the quarrel split the Ashkenazi community. In 1797 twenty-one members of Felix Libertate left the old congregation, the *Alte Kille*, and founded a new one, Adath Jesurun, the *Naye Kille*, which from 1799 prayed in the [Rapenburgersjoel](place:Rapenburgersjoel, the synagogue of the 1797 breakaway). The two sides fought it out in Yiddish pamphlets. In 1798 two Amsterdam Jews, Hermannus Leonard Bromet and the physician Hartog de Hartog de Lémon, were elected to the National Assembly, the first Jews to sit in any national parliament in Europe.

For the poor majority little changed at first. The guilds were abolished, but poverty was deep, and the first to benefit were the well-to-do.

## A Dutch Jewish church

The kings who followed wanted the Jews organised like any other church. In 1808 King Louis Napoleon made the two Amsterdam Ashkenazi congregations reunite and created an Upper Consistory for all the country's Jews, chaired by the lawyer Jonas Daniël Meijer (1780 to 1834), after whom the square by the Ashkenazi synagogues is named. It was deeply unpopular and ended with the French annexation of 1810. In 1814 King William I set up a new body, the Israelite Denomination, under a state committee: all congregations in one national structure, every province with a chief rabbi, Orthodox in the synagogue but free outside it, and committed to the House of Orange and the Dutch nation. After the 1848 constitution separated church and state, it became independent; from 1870 it was the Nederlands-Israëlietisch Kerkgenootschap (NIK), with a separate Portuguese body, the PIK.

## From Yiddish to Dutch

Language was the key. A law of 10 May 1817 ruled that in Jewish schools for the poor only Dutch and Hebrew would be spoken. At first that was impossible: the teachers themselves spoke Yiddish or German. Only once Dutch Jewish teachers had been trained and Dutch schoolbooks written, around 1835, did the inspectors enforce it, with the threat of losing the subsidy. In 1836 the Dutch Israelite Seminary was founded to train rabbis and teachers at home; from 1839 it stood in the Rapenburgerstraat, next door to the old breakaway synagogue. Western Yiddish died out within a century, leaving its words in Amsterdam speech.

The next step was the state school. After the education act of 1857, in force from 1861, the government stopped paying for denominational schools. The Jewish community chose not to keep its own: Jewish children went to the state schools, with Jewish lessons after hours. By 1915 only three Jewish day schools were left in the country. In 1865 the *Nieuw Israëlietisch Weekblad* was founded, and is still published.

## A new elite

The liberal constitution of 1848 opened politics. In 1849 the Amsterdam lawyer Michel Henri Godefroi became the first Jew elected to the Lower House, and in 1860 the first Jewish minister, of justice. The banker Abraham Carel Wertheim (1832 to 1897), head of the firm [Wertheim & Gompertsz](place:Wertheim & Gompertsz, a bank for American railway shares), sat in the provincial States and the Senate and chaired the Ashkenazi congregation from 1886 until his death, although he kept neither kosher nor the Sabbath. "In the church," he said, meaning the synagogue, "we are Israelites; outside it we must be, in the full, wholehearted, indivisible sense of the word, fellow citizens." The [park and fountain](place:Wertheim fountain (Wertheimpark)) named after him date from 1898. His friend [Samuel Sarphati](doc:Samuel Sarphati), a Portuguese Jewish doctor, gave the city its rubbish collection, a bread factory, the Paleis voor Volksvlijt and the plan for the Pijp.

Jewish entrepreneurs built some of the city's best-known shops: [De Bijenkorf](place:De Bijenkorf on the Dam), which began as a haberdashery on the Nieuwendijk in 1870; the fashion house [Gerzon](place:Gerzon's fashion works, where a thousand seamstresses sewed); and the design store [Metz & Co](place:Metz & Co, the New York building and Rietveld's glass room on the roof), whose Jewish owner lost the business in 1941. Diamonds, and the union that grew out of them, were the great Jewish industry: see [Diamonds and the ANDB](doc:Diamonds and the ANDB).

## Leaving the old quarter

From the end of the nineteenth century families who could afford it moved out of the Jodenbuurt: to the Plantage and the Weesperbuurt, then to the new working-class and middle-class districts of Oost, and between the wars to Zuid. In the Rivierenbuurt the Jewish population grew from about 4,000 in 1930 to some 17,000 in 1940, many of them refugees from Germany. New synagogues followed, from the [Obrechtsjoel](place:Obrechtsjoel (Raw Aron Schuster Synagogue)) of 1928 to the [Lekstraat synagogue](place:Lekstraat synagogue) of 1937 (see [Synagogues of Amsterdam](doc:Synagogues of Amsterdam)). The old quarter itself was being cleared: see [The Jodenbuurt and its clearance](doc:The Jodenbuurt and its clearance).

Not everyone who came was moving up. From 1881 tens of thousands of Jews fleeing pogroms in the Russian Empire passed through Amsterdam and Rotterdam, most on their way to America. Those who stayed kept to themselves, spoke Yiddish and opened their own prayer houses, such as the [Russensjoel](place:The Russensjoel and the old "Jodenkerkstraat" (Nieuwe Kerkstraat 149)) of 1889 and the [Blasiussjoel](place:Blasiussjoel, Swammerdamstraat 70) by the Amstel.

## How far did it go?

Integration went further in the Netherlands than in most of Europe. Jews did not form a political party of their own: the well-off tended to vote liberal, workers socialist. Political antisemitism never took root, and when a Limburg weekly, *De Talmudjood*, tried it in 1890, its publisher was convicted of libel. Zionism remained small; the Dutch Zionist League, founded in 1899, never counted more than one per cent of Dutch Jews as members.

Yet differences remained. Jews tended to live in the same neighbourhoods, to work in the same trades, to marry each other, and to have their own clubs, sports associations, choirs and charities; the [clubhouse built in 1876 for a Jewish men's choir](place:Plancius, the choir's clubhouse (Verzetsmuseum)) still has its Star of David. Social prejudice, in schools, clubs and offices, was common. Historians describe Dutch Jews before the war as "Dutch among the Dutch and Jewish among the Jews". In the 1920s and 1930s distinctions between Jews and non-Jews still worked to the non-Jews' advantage. After 1940 the occupier would use the registers, card indexes and addresses of a well-ordered society to mark Jews as Jews again: see [Persecution, 1940 to 1943, and the Jewish Council](doc:Persecution, 1940 to 1943, and the Jewish Council).
