Most of this map is about the past: a community that grew over three and a half centuries and was largely destroyed in three years. But Jewish Amsterdam did not end in 1945. It is smaller, it lives mostly further south, and much of it happens out of sight of visitors, but it has synagogues of every stream, its own institutions and its own calendar. This guide describes it at the level of public, institutional information. For how the community rebuilt itself after the war, see [Jewish Amsterdam, four centuries](doc:Jewish Amsterdam, four centuries).

## How many, and where

There is no register of who is Jewish in the Netherlands, and most Jews are not members of a congregation. The most careful estimate comes from surveys commissioned by Joods Maatschappelijk Werk, the Jewish social welfare agency, and carried out by the demographic institute NIDI. For 2010 they counted about 53,000 people: some 37,000 Jews by religious law (with a Jewish mother, or converted) and some 16,000 with only a Jewish father. Nearly half of them lived in the Amsterdam area. Before the war, more than 77,000 people were registered as Jews in the city alone.

After 1945 Jewish families moved from the old centre and the prewar districts of Zuid to Buitenveldert and Amstelveen, and that is where many Jewish institutions, shops and schools are now. Some of the historic synagogues in Zuid and the Pijp are still in use.

## The congregations

The largest is the Nederlands-Israëlietische Hoofdsynagoge (NIHS), the Ashkenazi community, which also calls itself the Joodse Gemeente Amsterdam. It describes itself as the largest Orthodox Jewish community in the Netherlands, with "Torah, Tradition and Tolerance" as its watchwords, and it is part of the national Ashkenazi body, the Nederlands Israëlitisch Kerkgenootschap. Its members range from strictly Orthodox to very liberal in their own practice, and under its roof are ten synagogue communities. It runs the ritual baths, the rabbinical court and the supervision of kosher food, and it manages the cemeteries at [Diemen](place:Jewish cemetery Diemen, where the Zeeburg graves went) and [Muiderberg](place:Jewish cemetery Muiderberg, the largest still in use), among others. Its office is in Amstelveen.

![The entrance of the Raw Aron Schuster Synagogue (Obrechtsjoel), Harry Elte's synagogue of 1928](commons:File:Ingangspartij van de Raw Aron Schuster Sjoel te Amsterdam - Amsterdam - 20409330 - RCE.jpg)

Three of its synagogues are historic buildings on this map. The [Obrechtsjoel](place:Obrechtsjoel (Raw Aron Schuster Synagogue)) of 1928, renamed after the postwar chief rabbi Aron Schuster, is the largest Ashkenazi synagogue still in use in the city and the one where most Jewish weddings take place. The small [Gerard Dou Synagogue](place:Gerard Dou Synagogue) of 1892 in the Pijp held the first Ashkenazi service in Amsterdam after the liberation, on 19 May 1945. At the [Lekstraat synagogue](place:Lekstraat synagogue) of 1937 the big hall is let for other uses, but the small shul in the side wing is in daily use.

The Portuguese Jewish community, the Portugees-Israëlietische Gemeente, is the congregation Talmud Torah, formed in 1639 and the oldest in the city (see [The Portuguese Nation](doc:The Portuguese Nation)). It still prays in the [Esnoga](place:Portuguese Synagogue (Esnoga)) of 1675, by candlelight, and in winter in the hall of the Ets Haim seminary next door, whose library is on UNESCO's Memory of the World register. Its dead are still buried at [Beth Haim](place:Beth Haim, the Portuguese Jews' cemetery at Ouderkerk) in Ouderkerk.

The Liberaal Joodse Gemeente (LJG), founded in 1932, is part of the Dutch union for progressive Judaism and of the World Union for Progressive Judaism. Men and women have equal roles in its services, and it has close to 2,000 members. Since 2010 it has met in a [new synagogue](place:The Liberal Jewish synagogue on Jacob Soetendorp's island) on a small peninsula by the Zuidelijke Wandelweg, with two prayer halls, a library and classrooms; the congregation calls it the largest synagogue in the Benelux.

![The Liberal Jewish synagogue of 2010 by the Zuidelijke Wandelweg](commons:File:Amsterdam Liberaal Joodse Gemeente Buitenzijde 4522.jpg)

The progressive congregation Beit Ha'Chidush has held its services since 1997 in the [Uilenburgersjoel](place:Uilenburgersjoel, the synagogue behind the wall) of 1766, on the old Jewish island of Uilenburg. There are also several smaller congregations, Orthodox, Chassidic and other, most of them in Buitenveldert and Amstelveen; they are part of the community's everyday life rather than places to visit.

The working synagogues are not museums. Most can be seen from the street; the Esnoga is open to visitors except on the Sabbath and holidays, and the others are seen by attending a service or on special occasions. Jewish holidays also change opening hours elsewhere: Beth Haim, for example, is closed on Saturdays and Jewish holidays.

## Institutions

The Jewish Cultural Quarter (Joods Cultureel Kwartier, JCK) brings together the [Jewish Museum](place:Jewish Museum (Joods Museum), four synagogues in one) with its children's museum, the Portuguese Synagogue, the [Hollandsche Schouwburg](place:Hollandsche Schouwburg, from theatre to deportation centre) and the [National Holocaust Museum](place:National Holocaust Museum (Nationaal Holocaustmuseum), in the old teacher-training college), which opened in its rebuilt form in March 2024. The Jewish Museum and the Portuguese Synagogue share a ticket; the memorial in the Hollandsche Schouwburg is free. The Jewish Museum began in 1932 in a tower room of the [Waag](place:Waag on the Nieuwmarkt, city gate, guild house and first Jewish museum) and has been in the four Ashkenazi synagogues on the Jonas Daniël Meijerplein since 1987. With the University of Amsterdam and the Vrije Universiteit, the JCK runs a research institute named after the seventeenth-century rabbi [Menasseh ben Israel](doc:Menasseh ben Israel).

Across the Weesperstraat stands the [National Holocaust Names Monument](place:National Holocaust Names Monument (Holocaust Namenmonument)) of 2021, with a brick for each of some 102,000 Jews from the Netherlands who were murdered. The community's weekly, the *Nieuw Israëlietisch Weekblad*, has appeared since 1865. Joods Maatschappelijk Werk provides social care. For the war years behind many of these institutions, see [Persecution, 1940 to 1943, and the Jewish Council](doc:Persecution, 1940 to 1943, and the Jewish Council).

## Kosher food

The rabbinate of the NIHS supervises kosher bread, meat, shops, restaurants and caterers in Amsterdam and Amstelveen and publishes a list of them. Most kosher shops and restaurants are in Buitenveldert, around the Kastelenstraat, with a few elsewhere in Zuid and in Amstelveen. In the centre, the café of the Jewish Museum is kosher and open without a museum ticket.

One name stands for the old Amsterdam sandwich: Sal Meijer, the kosher sandwich shop whose corned beef rolls were an institution for more than sixty years, closed in 2019 for lack of reliable kosher supervision and rising costs. Its sandwiches have since come back, in a kosher food court in Amstelveen.

## Through the year

Two public dates stand out. On the last Sunday of January, close to International Holocaust Remembrance Day on 27 January, the Nederlands Auschwitz Comité holds the National Holocaust Commemoration at the ["Nooit meer Auschwitz" monument](place:"Nooit meer Auschwitz", the broken-mirror monument (Wertheimpark)) in the Wertheimpark, usually with a silent walk, speeches by survivors and the mayor, and wreaths. In the days around 4 and 5 May, the Dutch days of remembrance and liberation, the JCK's Open Joodse Huizen and Huizen van Verzet open private homes across the city for small gatherings where the story of former Jewish residents, or of resistance in the house, is told. The February Strike of 1941 is remembered every 25 February at [the Dokwerker](place:De Dokwerker and the February Strike) on the Jonas Daniël Meijerplein.

The Jewish year itself, from Rosh Hashanah in the autumn to Passover in the spring, is lived mostly in the synagogues and at home. For visitors, the simplest way to meet it is a service in one of the congregations that welcome guests, or a visit to the Esnoga.
