For about seventy years diamonds were the trade that fed whole neighbourhoods of Jewish Amsterdam. In 1906 nearly 30 per cent of working Jewish men in Amsterdam, and almost 10 per cent of working Jewish women, worked in the diamond industry. Out of it came the most remarkable trade union in Dutch history, and a building, the Burcht, that still stands on a street named after its leader.

## A trade the guilds did not own

Diamond cutting came to Amsterdam in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries and, unusually, was never fully closed to Jews. In 1748 Christian diamond cutters and polishers asked the city to set up a guild that would keep Jews out; the city refused. The work was done at home, often in attics, on mills turned by foot, later by horses, and after 1850 by steam. Most cutters rented a place at a mill from the owner of a workshop.

## The Cape time

Everything changed when vast diamond fields were found in South Africa. In the "Cape time" of about 1870 to 1873 a flood of rough stones reached Amsterdam, and with it a flood of work and money. Thousands of new workers, Jewish and not, entered the trade. Large steam-driven factories went up. The brothers Israël, Marcus and Hartog Boas, who had started in 1870 with horse-driven mills on the Nieuwe Keizersgracht and paid their cutters wages instead of renting them a mill, opened the [Boas factory](place:Boas diamond factory, once the largest in Europe) on Uilenburg on 29 June 1879, with 357 steam-driven mills, at the time the largest in Europe. In 1907 the brothers Joseph and Abraham Asscher opened their [model works](place:Asscher diamond works, where the Cullinan was cleaved) on the Tolstraat; in 1908 Joseph Asscher cleaved the Cullinan there, the largest rough diamond ever found. In 1911 the dealers moved from cafés and a rented hall into their own [Diamond Exchange](place:Diamond Exchange on the Weesperplein (Diamantbeurs)) on the Weesperplein.

![The Boas diamond factory on the Nieuwe Uilenburgerstraat, photographed around 1900](commons:File:Diamantslijperij Boas.jpg)

The trade had its own ranks. Cleavers split the rough stones, cutters gave them their rough shape, and polishers ground and polished the facets. Work came in waves, and a boom could be followed by months without income. The dust was a danger: Hartog Boas himself died of tuberculosis in 1894, a common illness among cutters. Diamond workers and their families lived across the city, in the old quarter, the Weesperbuurt, the Plantage and the new Pijp, where the small [Gerard Dou Synagogue](place:Gerard Dou Synagogue) of 1892 was known as the diamond shul.

![A boy turns the heavy wooden wheel that drives a diamond cutter's mill, Amsterdam, 1909](commons:File:Diamantbewerkers, SFA022809203.jpg)

## The union

In November 1894, after a strike, the diamond workers founded the Algemene Nederlandse Diamantbewerkersbond, the ANDB. Its founders included Henri Polak (1868 to 1943), a diamond cutter's son from the Jodenbuurt, and Jan van Zutphen. Under Polak's chairmanship it became the first modern trade union in the country: it organised every grade of worker in one body, collected high dues, kept strike and unemployment funds, and negotiated with the employers as an equal. It won a minimum wage and insurance, a week of paid holiday, and in 1911 the eight-hour day. A large majority of its members were Jewish, which gave the ANDB a Jewish character, although it was a socialist and not a religious organisation. The same year, 1894, Polak was among the founders of the Social Democratic Workers' Party (SDAP), and many of its Amsterdam members were Jewish diamond workers.

Polak wanted more than wages. The ANDB ran a library, courses and outings, and published its own weekly. In 1899 he asked H.P. Berlage, who was then building the Exchange on the Damrak, to design a union building in the garden of a theatre in the Plantage. [De Burcht](place:De Burcht (diamond workers' union building)), "the fortress", finished in 1900, has battlements like an Italian town hall and murals by Richard Roland Holst; one shows the worker's ideal day of eight hours' work, eight hours' leisure and eight hours' sleep. The street, then the Plantage Franschelaan, has been called the Henri Polaklaan since 1945. His life is told in [Henri Polak](doc:Henri Polak).

![An open-air meeting of diamond workers in the Parktuin of the Plantage, 1916](commons:File:Openluchtmeeting van diamantbewerkers in de Parktuin van de Plantage in Amsterdam, Nederland 1916, SFA022809145.jpg)

Socialism did not come easily to the Jewish poor, who were loyal to the House of Orange: a gang in the Jewish quarter that kept socialist newspaper sellers out of the streets called itself "Voor Oranje". But by the early twentieth century a large share of Jewish workers voted socialist, while many still married in the synagogue and were buried at Muiderberg or Diemen.

## Decline

The good years did not last. From around 1910 work moved to low-paid home workers in the Belgian countryside around Antwerp, and the Amsterdam trade lurched from crisis to crisis. By the late 1920s it had practically stopped. In 1935 only 5,000 of the 9,000 diamond workers were left, and more than half of them were permanently out of work. The [Boas factory](place:Boas diamond factory, once the largest in Europe) stopped cutting in the 1930s. Many families fell back on peddling and the markets.

## 1940 to 1945

The occupier dissolved the ANDB in 1941. Because diamonds mattered to the German war economy, the Reichskommissariat supervised the trade directly, and some Jewish diamond workers and dealers received temporary exemptions from deportation: about 500 finishers and 300 entrepreneurs, with their families more than 1,200 people. In September 1943 most of the rest were sent to Westerbork or the camp at Vught, and in 1944 the last groups went to Bergen-Belsen. Nearly all the men died; some of the women survived. Henri Polak died in Laren in February 1943. Abraham Asscher, co-chairman of the Jewish Council, was deported in 1943 and survived Bergen-Belsen; see [Persecution, 1940 to 1943, and the Jewish Council](doc:Persecution, 1940 to 1943, and the Jewish Council).

At the Diamond Exchange a memorial stone of 1950 remembers the trade's Jewish victims. After the war the industry never recovered in Amsterdam; Antwerp took its place. Gassan Diamonds, in the old Boas factory, and the Asscher firm still work in the city, and the Burcht can be visited on tours.
