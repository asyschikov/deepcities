When Amsterdam was liberated in May 1945, the Jewish city that had existed in 1940 was gone. About 60,000 of its Jews had been murdered. Those who were left came out of hiding addresses in the city and the countryside, back from the camps, or out of the half-protected world of mixed marriages. This essay follows them through the first postwar decades. What came before is told in [Persecution, 1940 to 1943, and the Jewish Council](doc:Persecution, 1940 to 1943, and the Jewish Council) and [Hiding, help and resistance](doc:Hiding, help and resistance).

## Who survived

The figures that the NIOD gives, after the historian Loe de Jong, are stark. Of the Jews deported from the Netherlands, a little over 5,200 survived. About 1,150 came back from Auschwitz and 19 from Sobibor; most of the others were freed from Theresienstadt or from the trains out of Bergen-Belsen. About 16,000 had survived in hiding, about 10,000 in mixed marriages, and a few thousand by fleeing abroad or through exemptions. Some time after the liberation there were perhaps 36,000 Jews in the Netherlands, against some 140,000 in 1941. About 4,000 to 6,000 of them were children.

## A cold welcome

Those who returned from the camps came back to a country busy with its own shortages and its own war stories, in which Jewish suffering had no special place. Many arrived at Centraal Station. In 2019 a [plaque](place:Centraal Station's two plaques: the cold welcome and the sergeant) was put up there, at the initiative of the architect Moshé Zwarts, who came back as a boy from Bergen-Belsen. It states that the government received the survivors in the station's bare rooms under the tracks, that the welcome was "extremely cold", and that this time the occupier could not be blamed.

Homes had been emptied and let to others. Lotty Veffer, a diamond cutter who had survived Vught, Auschwitz and a death march, came back to Amsterdam on 26 August 1945 to find strangers in her house; an aid office gave her a horse blanket, and she spent the first night on a bench on the Apollolaan. Since 2017 [that bench](place:Lotty's bench, Apollolaan) carries her story. Others had to fight for furniture, shops and businesses taken during the war, and for money and shares held by the robbery bank (see [Lippmann, Rosenthal & Co.](place:Lippmann, Rosenthal & Co., the bank whose name was stolen)). The historian Dienke Hondius has described how returning Jews also met open antisemitism and impatience: people wanted to look forward.

The city itself sent bills. Returning Jewish owners of houses on municipal ground lease were charged the ground rent for the years they had been away, with fines for late payment. A student found the files in 2013; the NIOD looked into the matter and concluded that the city could have waived the charges, and from 2014 the city paid the money back.

## The children

Several thousand Jewish children had survived in hiding, many of them without parents. In August 1945 the government set up a Commission for War Foster Children (OPK) to decide where children whose parents had not returned should grow up. It had a non-Jewish majority and the power to decide whether a child stayed with the family that had hidden it, Jewish or not. Jewish organisations, who wanted the children brought up in a Jewish setting, fought these decisions for years. A Jewish guardianship society, Le-Ezrath Ha-Jeled, took on the care of many children. In 1965 it opened a children's home on the De Mirandalaan; its [climbing sculpture](place:The climbing sculpture of the Jewish children's home) is still there.

## Thanks, and silence

In 1950 a committee of Jewish survivors gave the city a monument of thanks to the Dutch who had protected Jews, the [Monument van Joodse Erkentelijkheid](place:Monument van Joodse Erkentelijkheid, the thank-you nobody wanted next door). The mayor accepted it "with pride, but also with shame". It was disputed from the start: critics said it did not speak for all Jews and passed over the many who had not helped. It has moved three times since.

![The Monument van Joodse Erkentelijkheid, back on the Weesperplein since 2021](commons:File:Monument van Joodse Erkentelijkheid in Amsterdam (2022) 06.jpg)

The war that the Netherlands remembered in the first postwar years was mostly a story of occupation and resistance. The persecution of the Jews took a long time to find its place. The theatre where they had been assembled became a memorial only in 1962 (see [The Hollandsche Schouwburg, the crèche and the children](doc:The Hollandsche Schouwburg, the crèche and the children)). In 1965 the historian Jacques Presser, who had taught at the Joods Lyceum, published *Ondergang*, the first full account of the destruction of Dutch Jewry.

## Rebuilding

The community that remained was small and had lost most of its leaders, rabbis and teachers. Services resumed where buildings had survived: in the [Gerard Dou Synagogue](place:Gerard Dou Synagogue) in the Pijp the first service after the liberation was held on 19 May 1945. Many of the old buildings, from the synagogues of the Jodenbuurt to the [Glass Palace](place:The Glass Palace of the Joodsche Invalide) of the Joodsche Invalide, were too big for what was left and were sold or handed to the city. The old Jewish quarter itself, its houses stripped for firewood in the Hunger Winter, was largely cleared (see [The Jodenbuurt and its clearance](doc:The Jodenbuurt and its clearance)). Many Jewish families settled in Zuid and later in Buitenveldert and Amstelveen, where new schools and synagogues followed. The Liberal congregation revived under rabbi Jacob Soetendorp from 1954 and built its [own synagogue](place:The Liberal Jewish synagogue on Jacob Soetendorp's island) in 1966.

There were new beginnings in other forms too. The students who had carried children out of the crèche opened the cinema [Kriterion](place:Kriterion, the cinema the resistance students built) in November 1945, in a building that had belonged to a Jewish workers' club.

## Late recognition

Recognition of what the Dutch state and the city had done came slowly. Restitution of stolen property dragged on for decades. In 2024 the city and its transport company paid back what the tram company had earned on the deportations. In 2025 a NIOD study laid out the part the city's own services had played, and on 24 April 2025 the mayor, Femke Halsema, apologised on the city's behalf and announced a fund of 25 million euros for Jewish life in Amsterdam. For how the city remembers, see [Remembering: monuments, names and stones](doc:Remembering: monuments, names and stones).
