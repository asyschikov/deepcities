When the city allowed Jews to settle, it expected them to look after their own poor, and they did. From the seventeenth century to the twentieth, a dense web of Jewish brotherhoods, funds, orphanages, schools, hospitals and homes grew up in Amsterdam, most of them on the islands and canals east of the Amstel. Many of their buildings still stand, some with Hebrew inscriptions over the door. Almost all of the people living in them in 1943 were deported.

## Brotherhoods and orphanages

Charity in Jewish tradition, *tsedaka*, is a duty rather than a favour, and both communities organised it through societies, each with its own purpose, members and funds. The Portuguese founded Dotar in 1615 to give dowries to poor brides, so that girls without means could marry. In 1648 came Aby Jetomim, "father of the orphans", which at first paid families to bring up orphan boys and had them trained in a trade; in 1817 it opened a home of its own in the Jodenbreestraat, and from 1930 it was in a house on the [Plantage Middenlaan](place:Aby Jetomim, the Portuguese boys' orphanage (Plantage Middenlaan 80)). Mazon Habanot, for orphan girls, followed in 1734.

The Ashkenazim, poorer and far more numerous, did the same on a larger scale. Megadlé Jethomim, "bringers-up of orphans", was founded for boys in 1738; from 1865 its orphanage stood on the Amstel, where the Stopera is now. An orphanage for Ashkenazi girls followed in 1761, and from 1861 it was in the [Rapenburgerstraat](place:Jewish girls' orphanage of the Rapenburgerstraat), where the Hebrew texts on its front can still be read.

![The former Jewish girls' orphanage in the Rapenburgerstraat](commons:File:Rapenburgerstraat 171, Amsterdam.jpg)

The old were cared for in the same way. The Portuguese brotherhood Mishenet Zequenim, founded in 1749, ran a home for poor old men that from 1794 was at [Nieuwe Herengracht 33](place:Nieuwe Herengracht 33, the stones of the Portuguese-Jewish old men's home); two text stones over the door, one in Hebrew and one in Dutch verse, still ask heaven's blessing on those who feed the aged. After the death of Joseph Fernandes Nunes in 1788 his bequest opened the [Fernandes Nuneshuis](place:Fernandes Nuneshuis, a Portuguese-Jewish almshouse) in the Nieuwe Kerkstraat: six rooms for two old women each, with the community's nursery school on the ground floor. Women were active in this work too, raising money, running homes and caring for orphans.

The need was enormous. By the late eighteenth century 87 per cent of the Ashkenazi community, and more than half of the Portuguese, depended on some form of poor relief. The [Hoogduitsers](doc:The Hoogduitsers) describes the poverty behind those figures.

## Schools

Education was a religious duty, and both communities had schools long before the state did. The Portuguese Talmud Torah school opened in 1616, with six classes leading from the alphabet to the Talmud; its library, Ets Haim, is still in the Portuguese Synagogue complex. The Ashkenazim opened their own Talmud Torah in 1660, mainly for poor children, and in 1740 a house of learning, the [Beth Hamidrash Ets Haim](place:Beth Hamidrasj Ets Haim, house of learning with a synagogue at the back).

In the nineteenth century the state took a hand. From 1817 Jewish poor schools had to teach in Dutch and Hebrew instead of Yiddish, and after the education act of 1857 Jewish children mostly went to the city's state schools, with religion lessons after hours (see [Emancipation and assimilation, 1796 to 1940](doc:Emancipation and assimilation, 1796 to 1940)). Private generosity filled gaps the city left. In 1887 the banker George Rosenthal and his wife Sophie Rosenthal-May paid for a [nursery school](place:The bridge yard and the last of the Jewish nursery school) on Uilenburg for the children of the poorest families; after an extra storey it took 600 children. In 1917 the city's [central kitchen](place:The city's central kitchen for poor schoolchildren) for poor schoolchildren opened with a separate kosher kitchen upstairs, and in the 1930s the [Witte Olifant school](place:Uilenburg rebuilt, the workers' block and the Witte Olifant school) on Uilenburg served kosher meals for five cents.

In the autumn of 1941 the occupier forced Jewish children out of the ordinary schools. Within weeks the Jewish community and the city had to set up separate Jewish schools; the Witte Olifant became Jewish school no. 2, and the [Jewish Lyceum](place:The Jewish schools of the Stadstimmertuin (Joods Lyceum)), where Anne and Margot Frank were pupils, opened in the Stadstimmertuin. New teachers were trained in a year at a [Jewish Kweekschool](place:Mauritskade 23 and 24, the Jewish Kweekschool) on the Mauritskade.

## Hospitals and care

The Ashkenazi community opened a hospital in 1804, on the Rapenburg, and moved it twice before building the Nederlandsch Israëlietisch Ziekenhuis on the Nieuwe Keizersgracht in the early 1880s, with about 110 beds. Harry Elte added a nurses' home in 1914, on land given by Sophie Rosenthal-May, and in 1927 a [washing house](place:Washing house and nurses' home of the Jewish hospital) at the back, where the dead were prepared for burial according to Jewish rite. The Portuguese had their own hospital from 1834 in the Rapenburgerstraat, which moved in 1916 to a new building by Elte in the Plantage, now the [Henri Polaklaan](place:Portuguese Jewish hospital (Henri Polaklaan)). The same year a third, the [Centraal Israëlietisch Ziekenhuis](place:Centraal Israëlietisch Ziekenhuis (gone), Jacob Obrechtstraat), opened in Zuid for patients who wanted Jewish care in the new districts.

Until 1911 old and disabled Jews who needed nursing went to the city poorhouse. That year, after a pamphlet by the rabbi Meijer de Hond, the society De Joodsche Invalide was founded. It grew fast, raised money with lotteries, and on 19 September 1937 opened a new building by J.F. Staal on the Weesperplein, with a synagogue, a kosher kitchen and a festive hall. Amsterdammers called it the [Glass Palace](place:The Glass Palace of the Joodsche Invalide).

![The Joodsche Invalide, Nieuwe Achtergracht 100, around 1925, before the Glass Palace was built beside it](commons:File:Het ziekenhuis de Joodsche Invalide, Nieuwe Achtergracht 100 met links en rechts de Weesperstraat, Afb PBKD00181000002.jpg)

## 1943

During the occupation these institutions became traps. Their residents were registered, defenceless and at known addresses. In January 1943 the women of the Fernandes Nuneshuis were taken away, and in February the old men of Nieuwe Herengracht 33. On 1 March 1943 the occupier emptied the Joodsche Invalide: 256 residents and staff were deported, and most were murdered at Sobibor on 13 March. The same month the nearly hundred boys of [Megadlé Jethomim](place:Megadlé Jethomim and the Monument Joods Verzet) were taken; three carers went with them to Sobibor, and none came back. Isaäc and Sara Drukker, who ran Aby Jetomim, were murdered at Sobibor on 4 June 1943. The girls' orphanage of the Rapenburgerstraat was emptied the same year, and in August 1943 the Centraal Israëlietisch Ziekenhuis was cleared. The patients and nurses of the hospital on the Nieuwe Keizersgracht were deported too, and few returned. The wider story is told in [Persecution, 1940 to 1943, and the Jewish Council](doc:Persecution, 1940 to 1943, and the Jewish Council).

## After the war

The small community that survived could not keep most of the buildings. The Glass Palace went to the city, which used it as a hospital and, since 1979, for the municipal health service; the boys' orphanage on the Amstel was later pulled down, and the Stopera stands on its site. Care was rebuilt on a smaller scale. In 1946 Jewish social work was brought together in Joods Maatschappelijk Werk (JMW), which still exists. The guardianship society Le-Ezrath Ha-Jeled, one of whose founders was the psychiatrist Hans Keilson, took on the many Jewish orphans and children who had survived in hiding, and in 1965 opened a children's home in Buitenveldert, of which only a [climbing sculpture](place:The climbing sculpture of the Jewish children's home) remains. The Centraal Israëlietisch Ziekenhuis reopened as the city's one Jewish hospital and served until 1978, when a Jewish wing opened in the hospital in Amstelveen. The children's own story is in [The Hollandsche Schouwburg, the crèche and the children](doc:The Hollandsche Schouwburg, the crèche and the children) and [Return and the postwar years](doc:Return and the postwar years).
