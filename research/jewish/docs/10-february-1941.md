In February 1941 Amsterdam did something no other occupied city in Europe did: it went on strike, openly and in large numbers, against the persecution of its Jews. The strike lasted two days and was crushed. It is still remembered every year, on 25 February, at the statue of the Dokwerker on the Jonas Daniël Meijerplein. This essay tells what led up to it, what happened, and where to see it in the city. The wider story of the persecution is in [Persecution, 1940 to 1943, and the Jewish Council](doc:Persecution, 1940 to 1943, and the Jewish Council).

## Fights in the Jewish quarter

In the first weeks of 1941 the WA, the uniformed fighting arm of the Dutch Nazi party NSB, went looking for trouble in Amsterdam. Its men hung "Jews not wanted" signs in cafés and hotels, pulled Jewish passengers off trams, and marched through the Jewish quarter, smashing windows and forcing their way into homes. Young men from the quarter, joined by workers from the Jordaan and Kattenburg, fought back.

On the evening of Tuesday 11 February a fight on the Waterlooplein left a WA man, Hendrik Koot, badly injured; he died a few days later. The German authorities made the most of it, with invented stories of what had been done to him. The next morning the old Jewish quarter was sealed off: bridges were raised and streets closed with barbed wire, and signs went up reading "Judenviertel / Joodsche Wijk". That same day Hans Böhmcker, the occupier's representative in the city, ordered the founding of a Jewish Council, which was to pass the occupier's orders on to the Jewish population.

On 19 February German police forced their way into Koco, an ice-cream parlour on the Van Woustraat in the Pijp run by two refugees from Germany, Ernst Cahn and Alfred Kohn. Regular customers had formed a defence group after earlier attacks, and the police were sprayed with ammonia gas. Everyone present was arrested. Cahn was shot in the dunes near The Hague on 3 March 1941; Kohn died in Auschwitz. A stone beside the door of [the old parlour](place:IJssalon Koco, where the road to the February Strike began) tells the story.

## The raid

The answer came from the top: Himmler, the Reich Commissioner Seyss-Inquart and the SS chief Rauter decided to make an example. On Saturday afternoon 22 February and again on Sunday 23 February German police sealed off the Jewish quarter and seized young Jewish men in the street and in their homes, 427 in all according to the National Committee for 4 and 5 May. They were made to squat on the Jonas Daniël Meijerplein, beaten, and taken away in lorries. A photograph shows a group lined up against the wall of the [Tip Top theatre](place:Tip Top, the Jewish quarter's theatre (gone)) in the Jodenbreestraat.

![Men from the Jewish quarter against the wall of the Tip Top theatre in the Jodenbreestraat, 22 February 1941](commons:File:Tegen de muur van het Tip-Top Theater in de Jodenbreestraat te Amsterdam.jpg)

The men went to camp Schoorl, where eight were released, and from there about 390 were deported to Buchenwald and then to Mauthausen in Austria. Some were killed in the gas chamber of Hartheim castle. Only two of them survived the war. For Amsterdam's Jews the name Mauthausen became a byword for death.

## The strike

The city had been restless for weeks over unemployment and over workers being sent to Germany; on 17 February shipyard workers in Noord had already walked out. The banned Communist Party (CPN) saw its chance. On the evening of Monday 24 February about 250 people, many of them city workers, gathered on the Noordermarkt beside the [Noorderkerk](place:Noorderkerk, where the February strike was called). Speakers, among them the sanitation worker [Dirk van Nimwegen](place:Dirk van Nimwegen plaque (February Strike)), called for a strike. Willem Kraan, a street paver who had seen the raid on the Nieuwmarkt, cycled round the city with his friend Piet Nak to call out their colleagues; the bust on the [Willem Kraanstraat](place:De Antifascist (Leo Braat)) is dedicated to him. That night the CPN wrote and duplicated its call, "Staakt! Staakt! Staakt!", and handed it out at the factory gates at dawn.

![The CPN pamphlet calling for the strike: "Strike! Strike! Strike!"](commons:File:Pamflet Februaristaking 1941.jpg)

On the morning of Tuesday 25 February the trams stayed in their depots, and without trams many people could not get to work anyway. City services stopped; shipyards, metal works, textile firms and department stores followed. In the afternoon crowds gathered in the streets. On the 26th the strike spread to the Zaan towns, Haarlem and Velsen, Hilversum, Utrecht and Weesp.

![Strikers on the Raamplein, Amsterdam, 25 February 1941](commons:File:Februaristaking Raamplein Amsterdam 25 februari 1941.jpg)

## The price

On 26 February the German general Friedrich Christiansen took over authority in the province and ordered that crowds be fired on with live ammunition. Nine people were killed and dozens wounded. By the 27th the strikers were back at work. In the weeks that followed hundreds were arrested, most of them communists; eighteen men were executed and others got long prison sentences. Kraan was shot in November 1942. The mayor was replaced by a man the occupier could rely on, Edward Voûte. The strike did not stop the persecution, and it was never repeated.

## How it is remembered

The city asked the sculptor Mari Andriessen for a monument. [De Dokwerker](place:De Dokwerker and the February Strike), a docker in work clothes, was unveiled by Queen Juliana in December 1952 on the square where the men had been rounded up; its base calls the strike "an act of resistance by the citizens". Every 25 February the strike is commemorated here; in 2026 for the 85th time, with speeches and a long procession of people laying flowers at the statue. The square behind the Jewish Museum still carries the neighbours' wish for a "Februaristraat", and the city has a Februariplein. Elsewhere, smaller memorials mark the people: the stone at Koco, the plaque at the Noorderkerk, Van Nimwegen's plaque in the Borgerstraat, and Lex Horn's strike mural of 1959 in the [bus garage in West](place:Garage West, Bons's birds and Horn's strike mural), painted for the city's transport workers. Nearby, on the Amstel at the corner of the Zwanenburgwal, the [Monument Joods Verzet](place:Megadlé Jethomim and the Monument Joods Verzet), the wish of the Jewish resistance boxer Bennie Bluhm, remembers the Jews who resisted.
