Baruch Spinoza (1632 to 1677) is one of the best-known philosophers of the Western tradition, and he was born and raised in the Portuguese Jewish community of Amsterdam. That community also expelled him. This short biography follows his Amsterdam years; for the community itself, see [The Portuguese Nation](doc:The Portuguese Nation).

## A merchant's son on Vlooienburg

Spinoza was born in Amsterdam on 24 November 1632. At home he was Bento, in the synagogue Baruch, and later, in Latin, Benedictus; all three mean "blessed". His family were former *conversos* from Portugal who had reached Amsterdam by way of Nantes. His father, Michael d'Espinosa, was a merchant who imported dried fruit and nuts from Portugal and served more than once as a warden of the congregation. His mother, Hanna Deborah, died in 1638, before he was six. Both parents lie at [Beth Haim](place:Beth Haim, the Portuguese Jews' cemetery at Ouderkerk) in Ouderkerk.

The exact house where he was born is not known. Tax registers of the early 1650s show the family renting a brick house on the corner of the Houtgracht and the Leprozengracht, at the edge of Vlooienburg, the island in the Amstel where most Portuguese Jews then lived. Both canals were filled in in 1882; the site is now covered by the [Mozes en Aäronkerk](place:Mozes en Aäronkerk, the church named after two houses). The synagogue of the united Talmud Torah congregation stood further along the same canal, and the island itself now lies under the town hall and opera house, the Stopera (see [Vlooienburg](place:Vlooienburg under the Stopera, the plaque that took thirty years, and Spinoza)).

He went to the community's school, where boys learned Hebrew, the Bible and its commentaries. At home the family spoke Portuguese; he also learned Spanish and Dutch, and later Latin, the language in which he would write. When his father died in March 1654, Baruch and his brother Gabriel took over the business. At about the same time he started to study Latin with Franciscus van den Enden, a former Jesuit who ran a school in the city, and to read the new philosophy of Descartes.

## The ban of 27 July 1656

On 27 July 1656 a *cherem*, a writ of excommunication, was read out in the synagogue on the Houtgracht against "Baruch de Espinoza". The text, in Portuguese, survives in the congregation's register of rules and resolutions, the *Escamoth*, now in the community's archive at the Stadsarchief Amsterdam (archive 334, inventory number 19).

![The writ against Spinoza in the Escamoth, the congregation's register of resolutions, 27 July 1656](commons:File:Ban of Baruch Spinoza, Amsterdam, 27 July 1656, 6 Av 5416.jpg)

The board, the *ma'amad*, states that it had long known of his "evil opinions and acts" and had tried by various means and promises to turn him from them. Instead it kept receiving reports of the "abominable heresies" he practised and taught and of his "monstrous deeds", confirmed by witnesses in his presence. With the consent of the rabbis it therefore excommunicated him and cut him off from the people of Israel. The text then curses him at length, and orders that no one may speak with him or write to him, do him any favour, stay under the same roof with him or come within four cubits of him, or read anything he had written.

It was the harshest ban the community ever issued, and it has never been lifted. It names no specific heresy. Most historians, following Steven Nadler, read it in the light of what Spinoza later published: that God is not a lawgiver who rewards and punishes, that the soul does not survive the body, and that the Law of Moses was not given by God for all time. A community of former *conversos*, still rebuilding Jewish life and tolerated by the city on condition that its members kept to their religion, could not let such views be taught in its midst. It had banned the freethinker Uriel da Costa before, and banned the physician Juan de Prado a year after Spinoza.

## After Amsterdam

Spinoza did not ask for the ban to be lifted. He left the business and lived among Christian dissenters and freethinkers, earning his keep by grinding lenses for spectacles, telescopes and microscopes. Around 1661 he moved to Rijnsburg near Leiden, in 1663 to Voorburg and around 1670 to The Hague. In 1670 his *Tractatus theologico-politicus* appeared without his name: a defence of the free reading of the Bible and of freedom of thought. He died in The Hague on 21 February 1677, aged 44. His friends published his *Ethics* and other writings the same year.

![Nicolas Dings's statue of Spinoza on the Zwanenburgwal, 2008](commons:File:Spinoza Nicolas Dings Zwanenburgwal Amsterdam.JPG)

## Spinoza in Amsterdam today

Nothing of his Amsterdam survives above ground. Since 24 November 2008 a bronze statue by Nicolas Dings has stood on the Zwanenburgwal, beside the Stopera, paid for by a citizens' foundation; its base carries the line "Het doel van de staat is de vrijheid" (the purpose of the state is freedom), after a sentence in the *Tractatus*.

The ban still stands. In 2012 the board of the Portuguese congregation asked its rabbi, Haham Pinchas Toledano, to look at it again; he decided to leave it in place. At a public symposium in Amsterdam in December 2015 he repeated that he had neither the authority nor the wish to lift it, because doing so would suggest that the congregation shared Spinoza's views on God.
