Samuel Sarphati (1813 to 1866) was a physician who decided that the illnesses he saw among the poor of Amsterdam had causes he could fight in the streets: filth, bad bread, bad housing and lack of work. In a little over twenty years he started a string of companies and institutions that changed the city. He became a public figure of the whole city in the decades after Jews gained equal citizenship (see [Jewish Amsterdam, four centuries](doc:Jewish Amsterdam, four centuries)).

![Samuel Sarphati, painted by Sybrand Altmann, 1860](commons:File:SamuelSarphati.jpg)

## Pharmacist's boy and doctor to the poor

Sarphati was born in Amsterdam on 31 January 1813, the son of the tobacco merchant Emanuel Sarphati and Reyna Musafia, in a family of the Portuguese Jewish community (see [The Portuguese Nation](doc:The Portuguese Nation)). At thirteen his father apprenticed him to the pharmacist Coronel on the Joden Houtgracht. He went on to study medicine in Leiden and took his doctor's degree in June 1839.

In 1840 he became one of the physicians to the poor of the Portuguese Jewish community, working with its hospital in the Rapenburgerstraat. It was the time when half the children in the city died before the age of five, and patients with scarlet fever, smallpox or measles were not admitted to hospital at all. He stayed a few years and then resigned. In 1843 he married Abigaël Mendes de Leon, from a wealthy Portuguese Jewish family, and the couple settled in a canal house on the Herengracht, [no. 598](place:Herengracht 598, Samuel Sarphati's house), next to the corner with the Amstel.

## Cleaning up the city

He began with things that touched public health. In 1842 he was among the founders of the Dutch society for the advancement of pharmacy, and in the mid-1840s he opened the city's first school for commerce and industry, in the Warmoesstraat.

Then came the refuse. Household rubbish, ash and street dirt were a nuisance and a health hazard. Sarphati set up a company for agriculture and land reclamation that would collect them and sell them as manure, and in August 1847 it won the concession for refuse collection in Amsterdam. The city took the work over in 1877.

The next target was bread, the staple of the poor, which was dear and often adulterated. Once the tax on flour and bread was lifted, Sarphati founded a company for flour and bread factories in 1855. Its works on the old city paving yard by the Vijzelgracht, with a steam mill and hot-air ovens, began baking in 1857 and sold bread about thirty per cent below the bakers' price. Other bread factories soon followed. The factory is gone; the [Nieuwe Weteringstraat](place:Sarphati's bread factory (gone), Nieuwe Weteringstraat) was laid out across its ground.

## A palace for industry

In 1852 Sarphati founded the Vereeniging voor Volksvlijt, a society to promote Dutch industry and crafts, and set out to give it a building like London's Crystal Palace. The Paleis voor Volksvlijt, a hall of iron and glass by Cornelis Outshoorn on the old drill ground of the Frederiksplein, opened on 16 August 1864. For sixty-five years it held exhibitions, concerts and meetings; it burned down in April 1929, and the central bank now stands on the site (see [Frederiksplein](place:Frederiksplein, where the glass palace stood)).

![The Paleis voor Volksvlijt on the Frederiksplein, photographed by Jacob Olie in 1892](commons:File:Paleis voor Volksvlijt Jacob Olie.jpg)

To pay for his plans he founded or co-founded financial companies, among them a national mortgage bank (1864) and the Nederlandsche Crediet- en Depositobank. He drew up a plan for a new quarter around the palace and along the Amstel, with a boulevard, now the Sarphatistraat, a first-class hotel and housing for working people. The city council adopted only part of it. The hotel came: Sarphati set up the company for the [Amstel Hotel](place:Sarphati's Amstel Hotel and the masseur who saved it), designed by Outshoorn, and building began in the spring of 1866. It opened in 1867 (see also [Sarphati's Amstel end](place:Sarphati's Amstel end, the grand hotel and the diamond trade)).

## Death and memory

Abigaël died in May 1864, a few months before the palace opened. Sarphati died on 23 June 1866, aged 53, before the hotel was finished. On 25 June his funeral left Herengracht 598 for Ouderkerk, and thousands lined the streets. He was buried at [Beth Haim](place:Beth Haim, the Portuguese Jews' cemetery at Ouderkerk) beside his wife.

In 1874 his friend, the banker A.C. Wertheim, launched a plan for a memorial. In 1886 a domed monument with a bronze bust was unveiled in the new [Sarphatipark](place:Sarphatipark and the Sarphati monument) in the Pijp, inscribed "Sarphati, Stichter van 't Nieuw Amsterdam" (founder of the new Amsterdam). In August 1942 the occupier renamed it Bollandpark and removed the bust; both came back on 18 May 1945. The former city workhouse on the Roetersstraat, now a nursing home, has been called the [Dr. Sarphatihuis](place:The city workhouse (Dr. Sarphatihuis)) since 1978.
