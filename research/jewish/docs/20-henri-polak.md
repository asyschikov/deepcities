Henri Polak (1868 to 1943) led the Amsterdam diamond workers for more than forty-five years. Under him their union, the ANDB, became the model for the Dutch labour movement, and he became one of the founders of Dutch social democracy. The trade itself is the subject of [Diamonds and the ANDB](doc:Diamonds and the ANDB); this is his life.

![Henri Polak around 1913, the year he entered parliament](commons:File:Onze afgevaardigden (1913) - Henri Polak.jpg)

## A cutter's son

Polak was born in Amsterdam on 22 February 1868, the eldest child of Mozes Polak, a diamond cutter who later set up as a jeweller on his own account, and Marianna Smit. He went to a Jewish primary school until 1881 and at thirteen began training as a brilliant cutter. By his background, his biographer notes, he seemed set to become a jeweller or a diamond dealer rather than a labour leader.

From 1887 to 1890 he worked in London. There he learned English, met Emily (Milly) Nijkerk, whom he married in 1888, and first heard socialism preached, from a street-corner speaker reading the Communist Manifesto. Back in Amsterdam, at a cutting works where he met the writer Frank van der Goes, he was drawn into the socialist movement. He joined the Sociaal-Democratische Bond in 1890, and in 1894 he was one of the twelve founders of the Sociaal-Democratische Arbeiderspartij (SDAP), the "twelve apostles".

## The union

In 1892 Polak helped revive a small diamond workers' society and edited its paper. The breakthrough came in November 1894, when Jewish diamond workers joined a strike. On 18 November 1894 they founded the Algemene Nederlandse Diamantbewerkersbond (ANDB), with Polak as chairman, a post he held until 1940.

He built a new kind of union. Members paid high dues; the union controlled who could enter the trade and how many apprentices were trained; it kept a strike fund and supported strikers in other trades. It came through lockouts of three and four months in 1902 and 1904, and it won the eight-hour day in 1911. It ran a weekly, lectures and a library. In 1899 Polak asked H.P. Berlage to design its headquarters, and in 1900 the union moved into [De Burcht](place:De Burcht (diamond workers' union building)) in the Plantage, where Richard Roland Holst's murals show the worker's ideal day of eight hours' work, eight hours' rest and eight hours' sleep. From the early 1900s Polak also led the international federation of diamond workers.

![Polak speaking to the staff of the Kimberley diamond works in the Valkenburgerstraat at the ANDB's 25th anniversary, 1919](commons:File:Viering zilveren feest van de A, SFA022809142.jpg)

The ANDB became the model for the Dutch trade union movement. On Polak's initiative the unions founded a national federation, the Nederlands Verbond van Vakverenigingen (NVV), and from 1906 to 1909 he was its first chairman.

## Politics, learning and beauty

In 1903 Polak became the first social democrat on the Amsterdam city council. In 1913 he entered the Senate, the Eerste Kamer, as the SDAP's first senator, and sat there, with one break, until 1937. He chaired the party three times.

He believed workers should be educated as well as better paid. In 1910 he set up the movement's central commission for workers' education. An admirer of William Morris, he held that industry was ruining the beauty of town and country. He worked for the preservation of windmills, monuments and nature, wrote *Het kleine land en zijn groote schoonheid* (The small country and its great beauty, 1929), and was a stickler for good Dutch. The University of Amsterdam gave him an honorary doctorate in 1932. Growing deafness drew him to quiet places: in 1906 Berlage designed a house for him in Laren, in the Gooi, where he spent his last years.

He never became a Zionist, feeling too attached to the Netherlands, though after 1917 he supported settlement in Palestine for Jews who were persecuted elsewhere. From 1933 he warned against National Socialism, and in 1936 he published *Het wetenschappelijk antisemitisme*, a rebuttal of "scientific" antisemitism.

## Arrest and death

The occupier arrested him in July 1940, among the first people it detained in the Netherlands. He spent about six months in the Amsterdam remand prison and was then held in Wassenaar, in a home run by a doctor of the Dutch Nazi party, the NSB. In July 1942 he was sent home to Laren, very ill. He died there on 18 February 1943, of pneumonia, aged almost 75. His wife Milly died in Westerbork in May 1943.

In 1945 the Plantage Franschelaan, where De Burcht stands, was renamed the Henri Polaklaan; the [Portuguese Jewish hospital](place:Portuguese Jewish hospital (Henri Polaklaan)) and [Jac. Baars's house](place:Jac. Baars's own house (Henri Polaklaan 11)) are in the same street.
