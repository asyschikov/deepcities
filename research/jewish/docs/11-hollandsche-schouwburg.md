Two buildings face each other across the Plantage Middenlaan, with a tram stop in between. On the north side is the Hollandsche Schouwburg, a theatre of 1892. On the south side stood the Jewish crèche, and next to it a Protestant teacher-training college, now the National Holocaust Museum. Between July 1942 and November 1943 tens of thousands of Jews passed through the theatre on their way to the camps. Across the road, a small group of people got several hundred children out. This essay tells that story; the people at its centre have their own essay, [Walter Süskind and Henriëtte Pimentel](doc:Walter Süskind and Henriëtte Pimentel).

## From theatre to assembly point

In 1941 the occupier renamed the theatre the Joodsche Schouwburg: from then on only Jewish artists could perform there, for Jewish audiences only. In July 1942, when the deportations began, the performances stopped. The building became the place where Jews who had been called up, caught in a raid or found in hiding were brought, registered and held, for hours, days or weeks, until they were taken, usually at night, to the trains for the transit camps of Westerbork and Vught. From September 1942 the raids became routine, and the theatre was often packed. On 25 March 1943 one prisoner counted about 1,300 people inside, with two men's and three women's toilets.

The Jewish Council had to staff the building. Its manager there was Walter Süskind, a German Jewish businessman living in Amsterdam. He kept the administration and dealt with the German guards every day; his position gave him room to make people disappear from the lists. Clerks such as Raphaël Halverstad, now remembered by a [bridge](place:Bridges with wartime names (Roetersstraat and Plantage Middenlaan)) in the Roetersstraat, did the same for children.

The last transport left the theatre on 19 November 1943, and the building closed as a deportation site.

## The crèche across the road

The crèche at Plantage Middenlaan 31 had opened in 1924. From 1926 its director was Henriëtte Henriquez Pimentel. In October 1942 the occupier made it an annex of the theatre: children up to about twelve were separated from their parents and kept there, because the theatre was no place for them.

From early 1943 Süskind, Pimentel and the resistance man Joop Woortman worked out ways to get children out. With the parents' consent, asked for each child in the theatre, staff took them from the crèche to hiding places outside the city. Four groups carried the children on: the NV of Woortman, the Utrechts Kindercomité, the Amsterdamse Studentengroep, in which Piet Meerburg was active, and the Trouw group. They found families, often far from the city, who would take a child in.

The tram stop helped. When a tram stood at the stop it blocked the guards' view of the crèche for a minute, long enough for someone to walk away with a child or to step onto the tram with one. Since January 2025 a [stumbling threshold](place:The stumbling threshold at the Artis tram stop (Plantage Middenlaan)) in the platform recalls that the same trams took tens of thousands to the station.

## The college next door

Next to the crèche was the Hervormde Kweekschool, a Protestant teacher-training college. Its head, Johan van Hulst, let the crèche staff bring children through the shared garden into the college. From May 1943 the small children took their afternoon nap in one of its classrooms; the caretaker watched the front door, and when the street was clear an escort left with a child as if from an ordinary school. Van Hulst was recognised by Yad Vashem as Righteous Among the Nations in 1972. Since 2018 a [bridge](place:Johan van Hulstbrug and its twin) by the Hortus carries his name.

How many children were saved is not known exactly. The National Holocaust Museum and the Jewish Cultural Quarter give about six hundred; other estimates run from five hundred to more than a thousand. The rescue worked only for some. On 26 July 1943 the children and staff of the crèche were taken away, and Pimentel was deported and murdered in Auschwitz. On 29 September 1943 the crèche was cleared for good. Süskind was himself deported in 1944 and died on a death march in February 1945.

## Becoming a memorial

After the war the theatre was rented out for parties and meetings, to the anger of many. In 1946 a committee was formed to buy it, and in 1947 the building was given to the city on two conditions: no more entertainment, and a place of remembrance with an eternal lamp. The auditorium was demolished; only the front was kept. The memorial was unveiled in 1962, with a column where the stage had been. Since 1993 a wall in the remembrance chapel lists about 6,700 family names of Jews deported from the Netherlands and murdered. In 2024 the front was restored to its 1960s colours. The Holocaust is commemorated here every year on Yom HaShoah, and on 24 April 2025 the mayor, Femke Halsema, apologised here on the city's behalf for its role in the persecution.

The college building opened as a temporary Holocaust museum in 2016 and, after rebuilding, as the [National Holocaust Museum](place:National Holocaust Museum (Nationaal Holocaustmuseum), in the old teacher-training college) in March 2024. A plaque of 1982 on its front thanks all who during the occupation helped to keep Jewish children from deportation. The crèche itself is gone; on its site stands Aldo van Eyck's [Mothers' House](place:Aldo van Eyck's Mothers' House (Hubertushuis)) of 1980. The theatre's old café next door, [Eik en Linde](place:Café Eik en Linde (Plantage Middenlaan 22)), is still a neighbourhood bar, and the cinema [Kriterion](place:Kriterion, the cinema the resistance students built) round the corner was founded in 1945 by the students who had carried children out.
