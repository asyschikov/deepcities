Etty Hillesum (1914 to 1943) was 27 when she began the diary that made her known around the world, forty years after her death. Her Amsterdam was small: a room in Zuid looking out over the Museumplein, a few streets around it, the offices of the Jewish Council, and the train to Westerbork.

![Etty Hillesum around 1939](commons:File:Etty Hillesum 1939.jpg)

## From Middelburg to Amsterdam

Esther (Etty) Hillesum was born in Middelburg on 15 January 1914. Her father, Louis Hillesum, an Amsterdammer, taught classical languages; her mother, Riva Bernstein, had fled the pogroms in Russia. The family moved with the father's jobs, to Hilversum, Tiel and Winschoten, and in 1924 to Deventer, where he became deputy head and later headmaster of the municipal grammar school. The occupier had him dismissed in November 1940. Etty had two brothers: Mischa, a gifted pianist, and Jaap, who became a doctor. She grew up with little Jewish religious practice.

In 1932 she went to Amsterdam to study law, and she took her degree on 4 July 1939. She then began to study Slavic languages, which she could not finish, and earned her living by giving Russian lessons. In March 1937 she had taken a room in the house of an accountant, Han Wegerif, at Gabriël Metsustraat 6, a building of 1901 on the edge of the Museumplein. She ran his household, and the widower and the young woman became lovers. She lived there until she left for Westerbork for good in 1943.

## The diary

In 1941 she met Julius Spier, a 54-year-old former banker who had fled Germany and worked in Amsterdam as a reader of hands and a therapist, with a small practice in his flat in the Courbetstraat. She went into therapy with him, and they too became lovers. Probably on his advice she began a diary on 9 March 1941, at the desk in her room on the Gabriël Metsustraat.

The diary begins as a record of her inner life: her moods, her loves, her reading, her search for a way of living. As the persecution closes in, the war takes more and more space. She refuses to hate, and on 23 September 1942 she writes: "And let us be aware that every atom of hate we add to this world makes it still more inhospitable." She wanted, she wrote, to be a chronicler of her time.

## The Jewish Council and Westerbork

In July 1942, through her brother Jaap, she got a job as a clerk with the Jewish Council, the body the occupier had forced on the community (see [Persecution, 1940 to 1943, and the Jewish Council](doc:Persecution, 1940 to 1943, and the Jewish Council)). Like all Council staff she was, for the time being, exempt from deportation. Soon she was moved to its department for social care of people in transit at Westerbork, the camp in Drenthe from which the trains left for the east. In her diary she called it hell, and she tried to help the people waiting there for transport. Her special status let her travel back and forth to Amsterdam several times.

She described the camp in dozens of letters. In 1943 the resistance printed two of them in secret, under a harmless-sounding title, *Three letters from the painter Johannes Baptiste van der Pluym (1843-1912)*, so that the Germans would not see what they were about.

She decided not to go into hiding. On 6 June 1943 she closed the door of the Gabriël Metsustraat behind her for the last time and went back to Westerbork. In July she lost her special status and became an ordinary inmate. On 7 September 1943 she was deported to Auschwitz with her parents and her brother Mischa. She was murdered there on 30 November 1943, aged 29. Her parents died on the journey or on arrival; Mischa died in Auschwitz in March 1944. Jaap was sent to Bergen-Belsen in 1944 and died on a transport of prisoners in 1945. None of the family survived.

## The diaries after the war

Before she left, Etty gave her notebooks to Maria Tuinzing, who had come to live in the same house, and asked that they go to the writer Klaas Smelik to be published if she did not return. Smelik received them in 1946 or 1947, but his efforts to find a publisher in the 1950s came to nothing. In 1981 the publisher Jan Geurt Gaarlandt brought out a large selection as *Het verstoorde leven* (An Interrupted Life). It was a success and has been translated into many languages; complete editions of the diaries and letters followed.

![Gabriël Metsustraat 2 to 6 in 2020: Etty Hillesum's room was at no. 6](commons:File:2020 Gabriël Metsustraat 2-6 (1).jpg)

Beside the door of [her house](place:Etty Hillesum's house (Gabriël Metsustraat 6)) a small plaque says: "In dit huis schreef Etty Hillesum haar dagboeken. 1941-1942" (in this house Etty Hillesum wrote her diaries). In 2020 the building was threatened with demolition. Neighbours and heritage groups protested, and on 17 June 2020 the city made it a municipal monument. In Deventer the Etty Hillesum Centrum keeps her memory.
