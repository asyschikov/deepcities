Walk through almost any older Amsterdam neighbourhood and you will step over them: small squares of brass set flush in the pavement in front of a door, each starting with the words "Hier woonde" (here lived), then a name, a year of birth and what happened to that person. They are Stolpersteine, stumbling stones. This essay explains how they come to be there, how many there are, and how to find the stones for someone you are looking for.

## What a stone is

The German artist Gunter Demnig began the project in 1993. His idea was to remember the victims of National Socialism one by one, not in one large monument but where they had lived. Each stone is a concrete block of 10 by 10 centimetres with a hand-stamped brass plate on top. It names one person and, in a few words, their fate, and lies in the pavement in front of the last home they chose for themselves, before they were forced to move, went into hiding or were taken away. The Amsterdam foundation stresses that they are not gravestones but stones of remembrance: they bring a family back together, symbolically, at the place where its life was broken off.

There are now more than 100,000 stones in over 1,200 places in Europe. In the Netherlands there are more than 16,000, in over 230 municipalities. Most are for Jews; there are also stones for other victims of National Socialism, such as Roma and Sinti and resistance fighters.

## How it works in Amsterdam

In Amsterdam the stones are requested through the Stichting Stolpersteine Amsterdam, which handles the requests and organises the laying. The stones themselves are made by hand under Demnig's supervision by his Stiftung Spuren, in six workshops, one of them in Amsterdam, at the Goethe-Institut on the Herengracht, which makes the stones for the Benelux.

Anyone can ask for a stone, but relatives come first. Requests from or on behalf of older relatives get priority, so that people over eighty can attend; if close relatives object, no stone is laid. A stone costs 150 euros. The city has given general permission for the stones; the present residents of the house get a letter about two weeks before the laying. City paving workers lay the stones, and relatives and neighbours often gather for a short ceremony.

Demand has outrun supply. At the end of 2024 the waiting list stood at about 1,700 requests. The foundation therefore stopped taking new requests for a time; the stop was lifted on 8 September 2026, and the waiting time is now one to three years.

## How many, and where

The foundation's own pages give more than 4,500 stones in Amsterdam, and close to 5,000 in its most recent count, with about 50 more each month. Among the earliest, in 2010, stones were laid for the Jewish members of the Dutch women's gymnastics team that won Olympic gold in 1928 in the [Olympic Stadium](place:Olympic Stadium), and for their coach.

The stones follow the map of where Jews lived in 1940 (see [Persecution, 1940 to 1943, and the Jewish Council](doc:Persecution, 1940 to 1943, and the Jewish Council)): thick in the old quarter and the Plantage, the Weesperbuurt, the Transvaalbuurt, the Rivierenbuurt and Oud-Zuid, scattered elsewhere. Where many people lived in one house, they lie in rows. Weesp, part of Amsterdam since 2022, has its own stones, at homes and at the old synagogue.

## Finding the stones for a person

- **The Stolpersteine app.** The app and website [stolpersteine.app](https://stolpersteine.app), made by the Stichting 18 September, shows stones across Europe on a map and lets you search by name, street and town. It is updated daily, and the Amsterdam foundation points to it as the place to look. If a person cannot be found there, you may assume no stone has been requested for them yet.
- **The foundation.** For Amsterdam you can also write to the Stichting Stolpersteine Amsterdam at info@stolpersteine-amsterdam.com, which is also where you request a new stone ([stolpersteine-amsterdam.com](https://stolpersteine-amsterdam.com)).
- **The Joods Monument.** The [Joods Monument](https://www.joodsmonument.nl) has a page for each of the more than 100,000 Jews from the Netherlands who were murdered, with their address, so you can see where a stone for that person would lie.

## Looking after them

Brass darkens over time, and a neglected stone can turn almost black and become hard to read. Volunteers and neighbourhood groups polish them; the foundation's advice is a brass cleaner of the Flitz type, such as Flintoline or Sidol, and never acid.

## Thresholds

A Stolperschwelle, a stumbling threshold, is a longer brass strip of the same make, 30 centimetres to a metre wide, with room for up to five lines. It names no one, but marks a place from which many were taken. In January 2025 Demnig laid three in Amsterdam, with the city and its transport company GVB, at tram stops from which Jews were taken to the trains: at the [Artis stop](place:The stumbling threshold at the Artis tram stop (Plantage Middenlaan)) on the Plantage Middenlaan, opposite the Hollandsche Schouwburg; on the [Victorieplein](place:Deportation threshold, Victorieplein), centre of the raid of 20 June 1943 in the Rivierenbuurt; and in the [Beethovenstraat](place:Beethovenstraat raids and tram stop) in Zuid. They followed research showing that the city's tram company had billed the occupier for these transports.

## Stones on this map

This map does not pin every stone. It pins places where the stones are the story, or part of it:

- [Twelve stumbling stones at one door](place:Twelve stumbling stones at one door (Nieuwe Prinsengracht 18)) on the Nieuwe Prinsengracht, for refugees, an old couple, their housekeeper and a widow who worked for the Jewish Council: one house, one cross-section of the Weesperbuurt in 1942.
- [The Brilleslijper house](place:The Brilleslijper house (Nieuwe Achtergracht 14)), with six stones, and none for the two daughters, because both came back.
- At no. 13 on the Jonas Daniël Meijerplein, near [De Dokwerker](place:De Dokwerker and the February Strike), three stones for the pharmacist Ephraim Rosenbaum, his wife and their baby son, murdered in 1943.
- [Meijer and Regina Turfkruier](place:Stolpersteine for Meijer and Regina Turfkruier) on the Herenstraat, two of the newest, laid in January 2026 with their great-grandchildren present.
- [Beethovenstraat 55](place:Beethovenstraat raids and tram stop), for the Pollack family of the Delicia lunchroom.
- In Weesp, the [cantor's house](place:Former cantor's house, Sleutelsteeg) in the Sleutelsteeg, and stones in the [Hoogstraat](place:Hoogstraat, south (nos. 32–62)), at [Binnenveer 11](place:Binnenveer 11 and its owners) and in the [Slijkstraat](place:Slijkstraat).
- And one place where residents chose otherwise: at Weesperzijde 141, instead of 39 stones for the residents of a Jewish boarding house, [a bronze of one of them](place:Leendert Pop and Pension Verdooner), Leendert Pop, stands for all.

## Questions

Not everyone likes the stones. In Munich the leader of the Jewish community, Charlotte Knobloch, objected that names of the dead would be walked on, and for years the city kept them off public ground. In Amsterdam the stones have broad support, and the waiting list shows how many families want one. For the wider story of how the city remembers, see [Remembering: monuments, names and stones](doc:Remembering: monuments, names and stones).
